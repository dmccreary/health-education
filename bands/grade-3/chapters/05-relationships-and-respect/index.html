    Relationships And Respect¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter examines the roles family members and peers play in healthy relationships, the right to personal boundaries, and how teasing, exclusion, or bullying affects individuals and the community. Students learn why treating all people of all abilities with kindness and respect matters.

    Concepts Covered¶

    Prerequisites¶

    This chapter builds on the foundational ideas about health introduced in Chapter 1: Foundations Of Health.

    Family, Friends, and Fairness

    Hi, friend! This chapter is about the people in our lives — the roles families and friends play, what makes a relationship healthy, and how everyone deserves kindness and respect. Let's think it through together. Healthy choices, happy you!

    Family Member Roles¶

    Every student belongs to a family, and every family looks a little different. Some families are large with grandparents and cousins nearby; some are small; some include stepparents, foster parents, or guardians. What all healthy families share is that each member plays a role in helping the family work well together.

    Family member roles are the different jobs and responsibilities that people in a family take on to keep each other safe, cared for, and supported, such as providing food and shelter, teaching new skills, or offering comfort during hard times. Grown-ups in a family often provide safety, rules, and guidance, while children contribute by helping with chores, following family routines, and treating others in the home with respect.

    Common family member roles include:

    • Caregiving — feeding, clothing, and looking after health needs
    • Teaching — showing a child how to do new things, from tying shoes to handling emotions
    • Providing safety — setting rules and routines that keep everyone secure
    • Emotional support — listening, comforting, and celebrating good news together
    • Contributing — even young family members can help through chores and cooperation

    No two families divide these roles in exactly the same way, and that variety is normal. What matters most is that the roles add up to a home where people feel cared for.

    Family roles give students their first experience of relationships, but family is not the only place students practice being a good relationship partner — school and neighborhood friendships matter just as much.

    Peer Roles In Relationships¶

    A peer is someone close to a student's own age, such as a classmate, teammate, or neighborhood friend. Just like family members, peers take on roles that shape how a friendship or classroom relationship feels.

    Peer roles in relationships are the different ways classmates and friends act toward one another — such as including others, sharing, cooperating, and offering support — that build trust between people who are growing up together. A student can be a good peer by inviting someone to join a game, sharing supplies, listening when a friend is upset, or working well as part of a team.

    Big Idea

    Peers can be a source of learning too — friends teach each other new games, new words, and new ways of solving problems. Good peer relationships go both directions: giving support and receiving it.

    Peer roles usually fall into a few familiar patterns:

    Peer Role What It Looks Like Why It Builds Trust
    Including others Inviting a classmate to join a game or group project Nobody feels left out
    Sharing Offering a pencil, snack, or supplies Shows generosity and fairness
    Cooperating Taking turns and following game or group rules Makes teamwork possible
    Listening and supporting Paying attention when a friend needs to talk Builds emotional safety

    Family roles and peer roles work together — the caregiving and teaching a student experiences at home often becomes the model for how that student treats friends at school.

    Healthy Relationships¶

    With family roles and peer roles in mind, students are ready for the bigger question: what makes any relationship — with a parent, sibling, friend, or classmate — a healthy one?

    A healthy relationship is a connection between people built on trust, respect, honesty, and kindness, where both people feel safe, valued, and free to be themselves. In a healthy relationship, disagreements can still happen, but people work through them without hurting each other physically or with unkind words.

    Signs of a healthy relationship include:

    1. Both people listen to each other, even when they disagree.
    2. People tell the truth, even when it's hard.
    3. Mistakes are followed by an apology and a chance to make things right.
    4. Each person supports the other's interests and feelings.
    5. Both people feel physically and emotionally safe.

    By contrast, an unhealthy relationship might involve one person often feeling scared, controlled, put down, or unsafe. Recognizing the difference helps students notice when a relationship needs a trusted adult's help.

    You now know how family and peer roles build healthy relationships. Next, this chapter looks honestly at what happens when relationships go wrong — through bullying and exclusion — so students can recognize it and know how to respond.

    Bullying¶

    Not every relationship stays healthy, and one of the most serious ways a relationship can go wrong is through bullying.

    Bullying is repeated, unwanted, and harmful behavior — physical, verbal, or online — by one person or group toward another person, usually where there is a real or perceived difference in power. Bullying can look like hitting or shoving, name-calling, spreading rumors, or repeatedly leaving hurtful comments online or in messages.

    Three features usually present in bullying:

    • It is repeated, not a single one-time conflict.
    • It is intentional, meant to hurt, control, or frighten someone.
    • There is a power difference, whether from size, popularity, group numbers, or other advantage.

    It is important for students to know the difference between bullying and an ordinary disagreement or conflict between friends, since not every unkind moment is bullying — but any repeated pattern of harm deserves a trusted adult's attention.

    A Serious Topic

    Bullying is a serious topic, and every student deserves to feel safe at school and online. If bullying is happening to you or someone you know, telling a trusted adult is always the right step.

    Exclusion¶

    Bullying is not the only way people can be hurt in a group. Sometimes the harm is quieter — being left out altogether.

    Exclusion is the act of deliberately leaving someone out of a group, activity, or conversation, such as refusing to let a classmate join a game, forming a group specifically to keep someone out, or ignoring someone on purpose. Exclusion can happen without a single unkind word being said, which sometimes makes it harder for adults to notice — but it can hurt just as much as name-calling.

    Exclusion can show up in ordinary school moments:

    • Saving seats so a particular classmate cannot sit with the group
    • Whispering so someone nearby is obviously left out of a conversation
    • Choosing teams in a way designed to leave one person out or picked last on purpose
    • Leaving one classmate out of a group message or invitation on purpose

    Everyone, regardless of ability, background, or how they look or communicate, deserves to be included. Exclusion does not always look dramatic — noticing who is not included in a game or conversation is just as important as noticing unkind words, and it is a first step toward stopping it.

    Effects of Bullying and Exclusion¶

    Bullying and exclusion are not small, forgettable events — they leave real effects on the people involved and on the whole community around them.

    Effects of bullying and exclusion are the emotional, social, and physical consequences experienced by a person who is bullied or excluded, which can include sadness, anxiety, loneliness, trouble sleeping, physical complaints like stomachaches, and lower confidence at school. These effects do not stay contained to just the person targeted — a classroom or school community where bullying or exclusion is common becomes a less safe, less trusting place for everyone.

    Effects can include:

    Who Is Affected Possible Effects
    The person targeted Sadness, fear, loneliness, trouble sleeping, stomachaches, avoiding school
    Bystanders who witness it Feeling unsafe, guilt, uncertainty about what to do
    The person doing the bullying or excluding Difficulty forming healthy relationships, ongoing behavior patterns without help
    The whole classroom or community Lower trust, less willingness to speak up, an unwelcoming environment

    This is exactly why treating all people — of every ability, background, and way of communicating — with kindness and respect matters so much. A classroom where everyone is included is a classroom where everyone can focus, learn, and feel safe.

    You've Got This

    Learning about the effects of bullying and exclusion can feel heavy, but understanding them is exactly what helps students choose kindness and know when to ask a trusted adult for help.

    Right to Personal Boundaries¶

    Every person — regardless of age — has the right to decide who can be close to them, what kind of touch feels okay, and what personal information they choose to share.

    The right to personal boundaries means every person has the right to say what feels comfortable for their own body, space, and belongings, and to have that boundary respected by others, including family members, peers, and adults. A boundary might be as simple as "please don't touch my things without asking" or "I don't want a hug right now, but a high-five is okay."

    Boundaries matter because they help a person feel safe, respected, and in control of their own choices. A few key facts about personal boundaries for Grade 3 students:

    • A person's boundary should be respected even if others don't understand it or think it's unusual.
    • Saying "no" to unwanted touch or attention is allowed, even with familiar people.
    • If someone's boundary is not being respected, telling a trusted adult is the right next step — asking for help is a strength, not a weakness.
    • Respecting other people's boundaries, even when a student disagrees with them, is part of being a good friend and family member.

    Helpful Tip

    A clear, calm sentence works well for setting a boundary: "I don't like that, please stop," or "I'd rather not, thank you." Practicing these words ahead of time makes them easier to use in the moment.

    Wrap-Up for Teachers¶

    By the end of this chapter, most Grade 3 students should be able to describe the roles of family members and peers in healthy relationships (3.2.1.1) and explain how teasing, exclusion, or bullying impacts individuals and the community, including why treating all people of all abilities with kindness and respect matters (3.3.1.6), as well as describe the right to personal boundaries (3.5.1.4). The MicroSims progress from Understand-level classification and scenario-interpretation activities (family and peer roles, healthy relationships, recognizing bullying, understanding ripple effects) to an Apply-level boundary-practice simulator, matching the Understand/Apply mix of benchmarks in this content strand. Because this is a K-3 band, keep the body text as teacher-facing read-aloud material and treat the MicroSims as the direct hands-on practice for students. Keep the tone plainly sincere — without puns or light dog-themed phrasing — specifically in the Bullying, Exclusion, and Effects of Bullying and Exclusion sections, since these are violence-prevention-adjacent topics; the mascot may still appear there warmly, but only in its neutral or encouraging voice. Always close any bullying, exclusion, or boundary discussion with a clear reminder that trusted adults are available to help.
