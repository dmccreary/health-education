    Wellness and Disease Prevention¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter covers common causes of allergic reactions and prevention strategies, and how cultural traditions such as dance and community sports provide physical activity benefits for the body and mood. Students also distinguish infectious from non-infectious disease, learn how germs spread, and study prevention strategies alongside cultural perspectives on wellness and caregiving.

    This chapter builds on Chapter 2: Growth and Development Across Cultures, which introduces respecting cultural differences, a concept this chapter's discussion of cultural physical-activity traditions and wellness perspectives depends on.

    Keeping Your Body Well

    Hi, friend! This chapter is about staying well — recognizing allergies, moving your body in ways that also lift your mood, and understanding how diseases start and how to prevent them. Healthy choices, happy you!

    Allergy¶

    Some bodies react strongly to things that don't bother most people at all — a certain food, a bee sting, or even pollen in the air. Understanding why this happens is the first step toward staying safe.

    An allergy is the body's immune system overreacting to a substance that is usually harmless, called an allergen, and treating it like a threat. Common allergens include foods (peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, shellfish), insect stings (bees, wasps), pollen, pet dander, and certain medicines. When a person with an allergy is exposed to their allergen, their immune system releases chemicals that cause symptoms.

    Allergic reaction symptoms can range from mild to very serious. Recognizing them quickly matters:

    • Itchy, watery eyes or a runny, stuffy nose
    • Hives (red, itchy bumps) or a skin rash
    • Swelling of the lips, face, or tongue
    • Coughing, wheezing, or trouble breathing
    • Stomach pain, nausea, or vomiting (often with food allergies)

    Most allergic reactions are mild and uncomfortable rather than dangerous. However, a small number of people can have a severe, fast-moving reaction called anaphylaxis, which affects breathing and requires emergency medical help right away.

    Serious Symptoms Need Fast Action

    Swelling of the face or throat, trouble breathing, or feeling like your throat is closing up are emergency signs. Tell a trusted adult immediately — these symptoms should never be ignored or wait.

    Allergy Prevention And Management¶

    Knowing that allergies exist is useful, but the practical skill is knowing what to do about one — either your own or someone else's.

    Allergy prevention and management means the everyday practices that reduce the chance of an allergic reaction and the steps to take if one happens anyway. Prevention starts with knowing your own allergens (or a classmate's) and avoiding them on purpose. Management means having a plan ready in case exposure happens despite your best efforts.

    The table below organizes prevention and response strategies students can actually use:

    Situation What To Do
    You know your own allergen Read food labels carefully; ask what's in a food before eating it at a party or friend's house
    A classmate has a known allergy Don't share food with them without checking; take their allergy seriously, even if you don't understand it
    Mild symptoms appear Tell a trusted adult right away, even if you're not fully sure it's a reaction
    Severe symptoms appear (swelling, trouble breathing) This is a medical emergency — get an adult immediately; some people carry emergency medicine (an epinephrine auto-injector) for exactly this situation

    Many schools have clear allergy plans, such as peanut-free tables in the cafeteria. These plans are not overreactions — they exist because for some students, exposure to even a small amount of an allergen can be dangerous. Respecting these plans is one concrete way you support a classmate's safety.

    Cultural Physical Activity Traditions¶

    Bodies are built to move, and every culture around the world has developed its own joyful ways of doing that. Recognizing these traditions helps you see physical activity as something richer than just "exercise."

    Cultural physical activity traditions are forms of movement — dances, games, and community sports — that come from a culture's history and are practiced and passed down as part of that community's identity. These are not simply exercise routines; they carry meaning, stories, and connection to family and community.

    Examples from around the world include:

    • Traditional dances performed at celebrations, such as powwow dances practiced in many Indigenous nations, Irish step dancing, or West African dance traditions
    • Community sports like lacrosse, which originated with Indigenous nations in North America and is still played today
    • Martial arts traditions, such as Tai Chi or Capoeira, which combine movement, rhythm, and cultural history
    • Community games passed down through generations, like jump-rope games with traditional rhymes, or traditional wrestling styles practiced in different countries

    These traditions are valid, valuable forms of physical activity — not just "extra" or "optional" activities. They belong in the same conversation as organized sports and gym class, and they often carry the added benefit of connecting a person to their family history and community.

    Movement Looks Different Everywhere

    What is a physical activity tradition in your own family or community? It might be a dance, a game, or a sport passed down from someone who taught you. That counts as real, meaningful physical activity.

    Benefits Of Physical Activity On Mood¶

    Physical activity does more than build strong muscles and a healthy heart — it changes how a person feels, often within minutes.

    Benefits of physical activity on mood refers to the way movement — including dance, sports, and active play — improves how a person feels emotionally, not just physically. When you move your body, your brain releases chemicals that can reduce stress, ease worry, and boost feelings of happiness. Physical activity can also improve focus and energy for learning afterward.

    Consider these mood-related benefits that regular physical activity provides:

    1. Reduces stress — Moving your body can help release tension built up from a hard day.
    2. Improves mood — Many people feel happier and calmer after being active.
    3. Boosts energy for learning — A short burst of movement can help you focus better afterward, not just tire you out.
    4. Builds confidence — Getting better at a dance, game, or sport over time builds a sense of accomplishment.
    5. Strengthens connection — Group activities like community dances or team sports build friendships and a sense of belonging.

    Cultural physical activity traditions offer all of these same mood benefits, plus something extra: a connection to family history, community identity, and celebration. Dancing at a family celebration or playing a traditional community game delivers the same mood-lifting chemistry as any other physical activity, while also strengthening cultural belonging.

    Infectious Disease¶

    Now that you've explored movement and mood, this chapter turns to a different kind of wellness topic: understanding how illness works, starting with the kind that can spread from person to person.

    An infectious disease is an illness caused by a germ — such as a virus, bacterium, or fungus — that can spread from one person, animal, or surface to another. Colds, the flu, strep throat, and chickenpox are all examples of infectious diseases. Because these illnesses are caused by living or semi-living organisms that can travel, they are the kind of illness you can "catch" from someone else or give to someone else.

    Not every infectious disease spreads the same way or causes the same symptoms, but they share one key trait: a germ is the cause, and that germ can move from one host to another.

    Non-Infectious Disease¶

    Not every illness comes from a germ, and not every illness can be "caught" from another person. This next category works very differently.

    A non-infectious disease is a health condition that is not caused by a germ and cannot spread from person to person. Examples include asthma, diabetes, and some forms of heart disease. These conditions can come from genetics (traits passed down in a family), environment, lifestyle factors, or a combination of causes — but never from catching a germ from someone else.

    Understanding the difference between these two categories matters because the prevention strategies are different. You cannot "catch" asthma from a classmate, and you cannot prevent diabetes the same way you prevent a cold. The table below compares the two categories directly:

    Feature Infectious Disease Non-Infectious Disease
    Caused by a germ? Yes No
    Can spread person to person? Yes No
    Examples Cold, flu, strep throat, chickenpox Asthma, diabetes, some heart disease
    Common causes Viruses, bacteria, fungi Genetics, environment, lifestyle factors
    Prevention approach Hygiene, vaccines, avoiding exposure Healthy habits, medical management, monitoring

    How Germs Spread¶

    Understanding that infectious diseases come from germs raises an important next question: exactly how does a germ get from one person to another?

    How germs spread describes the specific paths germs travel to move from one person, animal, or surface to a new host. Germs are tiny living things — far too small to see without a microscope — but they are very good at traveling. Common paths include:

    1. Direct contact — Touching another person, such as shaking hands or hugging, when germs are present on the skin
    2. Respiratory droplets — Coughing or sneezing releases tiny droplets containing germs into the air, which others can breathe in or touch
    3. Contaminated surfaces — Touching a doorknob, desk, or shared object that has germs on it, then touching your face
    4. Contaminated food or water — Eating or drinking something that germs have gotten into
    5. Insect or animal bites — Some germs travel through a bite, such as certain diseases spread by mosquitoes or ticks

    Understanding these paths is exactly what makes prevention possible — each path suggests its own defense, which is exactly what the next section covers.

    Disease Prevention Strategies¶

    Every pathway a germ can travel also suggests a defense. This section turns understanding into action.

    Disease prevention strategies are the everyday habits and practices that reduce the chance of catching or spreading an infectious disease. Because infectious diseases spread along specific pathways, most prevention strategies work by blocking one of those pathways directly.

    Here are prevention strategies matched to how they protect you:

    • Wash your hands often, especially before eating and after using the bathroom or blowing your nose — this blocks direct contact and surface spread.
    • Cover coughs and sneezes with your elbow or a tissue, not your bare hand — this blocks respiratory droplet spread.
    • Avoid sharing personal items like water bottles, utensils, or lip balm — this blocks direct contact and surface spread.
    • Stay home when sick, when possible, to avoid spreading germs to classmates and teachers.
    • Keep vaccines up to date — vaccines help your immune system recognize and fight specific germs before you get seriously ill.
    • Store and prepare food safely — this blocks the food and water pathway.

    Non-infectious diseases are prevented differently, since no germ is involved. Strategies for supporting overall wellness against non-infectious disease risk include eating a balanced diet, staying physically active, getting enough sleep, and following any medical guidance a doctor provides for a condition that runs in the family.

    Small Habits, Big Difference

    Washing your hands for the length of two rounds of "Happy Birthday" is long enough to actually remove germs. Try timing yourself next time!

    Cultural Wellness Perspectives¶

    Disease prevention and wellness are not understood the same way in every culture — and recognizing that variety is itself an important wellness skill.

    Cultural wellness perspectives are the different beliefs, traditions, and practices that cultures around the world use to understand health, prevent illness, and care for people who are sick. These perspectives are valid, time-tested approaches, not outdated ideas replaced entirely by modern medicine. Many families blend cultural wellness practices with the medical guidance of a doctor, and both can work together.

    Examples of cultural wellness practices found around the world include:

    • Herbal traditions — Many cultures have long histories of using specific plants and herbal teas to support wellness, such as ginger tea for stomach discomfort or chamomile for calm rest, knowledge passed down through generations.
    • Caregiving customs — Many cultures have specific traditions for how family and community care for someone who is sick, such as multiple generations gathering to help, special foods prepared for recovery, or specific rest practices.
    • Community-based healing practices — Traditions such as sweat lodge ceremonies in some Indigenous nations, traditional Chinese medicine practices like acupuncture, or community elder knowledge about wellness, all represent long-standing systems of care.
    • Spiritual and communal wellness practices — Prayer, meditation, or community gatherings that many cultures use to support emotional and physical wellness together.

    Respecting these traditions means understanding that "health" is not defined only one way. A caregiving custom from your grandparents' culture and a prevention strategy from a doctor's office can both be legitimate parts of staying well.

    Bringing this chapter together, wellness is bigger than avoiding illness. It includes recognizing your own body's signals like allergies, moving in ways that lift your mood — including through cultural traditions passed down in your family — understanding how disease works and spreads, and respecting that different cultures have valid, valuable ways of caring for one another. All of these pieces work together to support a whole, healthy life.
