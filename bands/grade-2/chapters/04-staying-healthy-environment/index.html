    Staying Healthy in Our Environment¶

    Summary¶

    This chapter introduces environmental health risks such as sun exposure, loud noise, unsafe chemicals, and unsafe water, and how to protect the body from them. Students learn about the value of physical activity, including cultural and traditional games or dances, and explore how germs spread through air, food, water, and personal contact along with the hygiene practices that prevent illness. After completing this chapter, students will be able to describe an environmental health risk, name a form of physical activity, and explain how illness prevention works.

    Concepts Covered¶

    This chapter covers the following 8 concepts from the learning graph:

    2. Sun And Noise Protection
    3. Unsafe Chemicals And Water

    Teacher Notes: How to Use This Chapter¶

    This chapter is written for you, the teacher, to read aloud, narrate, and guide from. Grade 2 students are building independent reading skills, but full understanding of environmental risks and disease prevention still depends on your read-aloud support and classroom discussion. Use the text below as a script while your class explores the pictures and MicroSims. The tone throughout stays warm and encouraging, since this chapter focuses on everyday protective habits rather than sensitive personal-safety content.

    Taking Care of Our Bodies and Our World

    Hi again, friend! Today we're exploring how our bodies and the world around us work together. We'll look at things in our environment that can hurt us, how to protect ourselves, why moving our bodies feels great, and how tiny germs spread — and how we stop them. Healthy choices, happy you!

    What Is an Environmental Health Risk?¶

    An environmental health risk is anything in the world around us — in the air, water, ground, or nearby objects — that could hurt our bodies if we are not careful. Environmental health risks are different from germs or foods; they come from the environment itself, like the sun overhead, a loud sound, a bottle of cleaning spray, or water that is not safe to drink.

    Second graders encounter environmental health risks almost every day, often without noticing them. Understanding what they are is the first step toward protecting the body. Common environmental health risks include:

    • Strong sunlight that can burn skin.
    • Loud noises that can hurt ears.
    • Chemicals, such as cleaning products or medicines, that are unsafe to touch, smell, or taste.
    • Water that has not been made safe to drink.

    Notice that these four risks come from very different places — the sky, a sound, a bottle, and a faucet — but they share one thing in common: each one can hurt the body if a person does not know how to protect against it. None of these things are "bad" on their own — the sun helps plants grow, and cleaning products keep our homes healthy! The risk only appears when we don't protect ourselves the right way.

    Sun and Noise Protection¶

    Sun and noise protection means using specific habits and tools to keep sunlight and loud sounds from damaging the skin and ears. These two risks are grouped together because both come from everyday parts of life — being outside and hearing sounds — that become safer with simple protective steps.

    Protecting skin from the sun:

    1. Wear sunscreen on skin that will be in the sun.
    2. Wear a hat that shades the face and neck.
    3. Wear sunglasses that block bright light.
    4. Sit in shade during the sunniest part of the day.

    Protecting ears from loud noise:

    1. Cover ears or wear ear protection near very loud sounds, like fireworks or power tools.
    2. Move away from a sound that hurts your ears.
    3. Keep headphone volume at a level where you can still hear someone talking next to you.

    Before trying the diagram below, think about this: sun protection and noise protection use different tools (sunscreen versus earmuffs), but they follow the exact same pattern — notice the risk, then choose a protection step before damage happens, not after.

    Helpful Tip

    A good habit is to put on sunscreen and a hat before going outside to play, not after your skin already feels hot. Protecting first is always easier than fixing later!

    Unsafe Chemicals and Water¶

    Unsafe chemicals and water refers to substances that look harmless — or even inviting — but can make a person very sick if touched, tasted, or swallowed. This includes cleaning products, medicines that belong to someone else, and water that has not been made safe to drink.

    Some important safety rules:

    • Never touch, smell, or taste a cleaning product, spray, or medicine without a trusted adult.
    • Many unsafe chemicals are kept in bottles that look like drinks or candy — always check with an adult first.
    • Not all water is safe to drink. Water from a pond, puddle, or unfamiliar source may contain unsafe chemicals or germs.
    • Only drink water that a trusted adult says is safe, such as from a home tap, a water fountain, or a sealed bottle.

    Comparing safe and unsafe water sources helps make this idea concrete:

    Water Source Usually Safe to Drink? Why
    Home kitchen tap Yes Treated and tested by the water system
    School water fountain Yes Treated and tested, checked by adults
    Sealed bottled water Yes Sealed and tested before sale
    Puddle or pond No Not treated; may carry germs or chemicals
    Unlabeled bottle found outside No Could contain an unsafe chemical, not water

    This table shows a pattern worth remembering: a water source is safe when a trusted adult and a water system have checked it — never based only on how the water looks.

    An Important Rule

    If you find a bottle, spray, or container you don't recognize, don't touch it, smell it, or taste it. Go tell a trusted adult right away.

    Physical Activity¶

    Physical activity is any movement of the body that uses energy and helps a person stay healthy and strong — running, jumping, dancing, swimming, biking, and playing active games all count. Physical activity supports the body in many ways at once.

    Regular physical activity helps the body by:

    • Building strong muscles and bones.
    • Keeping the heart and lungs strong.
    • Helping the body use energy from food (connecting back to what students learned about food and energy).
    • Improving mood and helping manage strong emotions (connecting back to Chapter 3).
    • Helping the body rest better at night.

    Physical activity does not have to mean a formal sport. Climbing on playground equipment, playing tag, riding a scooter, and dancing to a favorite song are all physical activity too.

    However you like to move — running, dancing, biking, or playing tag — your body benefits every time. Now let's look at some of the many ways different cultures move and play.

    Cultural Physical Activity¶

    Cultural physical activity means active games, dances, and traditions that come from a specific culture or community and have often been passed down for generations. These activities are physical activity and a way that communities share stories, celebrations, and belonging.

    Communities around the world have wonderful traditions of active play. A few examples:

    • Jump-rope games with rhymes and chants, played in many communities across generations.
    • Tinikling, a dance from the Philippines where dancers step between long bamboo poles in rhythm.
    • Powwow dancing, practiced by many Indigenous nations in North America, with regalia, drumming, and specific dance styles passed down through families.
    • Line dances and circle dances, found in many cultures, where a whole group moves together in patterns.
    • Traditional ball and tag games, played by children in cultures all over the world, each with its own rules and rhymes.

    No single tradition here is more "normal" than another — every culture has its own active games and dances, and all of them count as physical activity that also carries meaning and history for the people who practice them. Learning about these traditions helps us move our bodies and respect where they come from.

    Germs¶

    Germs are tiny living things — far too small to see without a microscope — that can enter the body and sometimes cause illness. Germs are all around us: in the air, on surfaces, in food, and in water. Most germs do not make us sick, but some kinds can.

    A few key facts about germs for Grade 2 students:

    • Germs are so small that thousands could fit on the head of a pin.
    • Germs can be picked up without a person knowing it, since they cannot be seen.
    • Not touching your face with unwashed hands is one way to keep germs from entering your body.
    • Washing hands, covering coughs, and keeping surfaces clean all help remove or block germs.

    You Can't See Them, But They're There

    You can't see germs, but that doesn't mean they aren't there! That's exactly why hygiene habits like handwashing matter, even when your hands look clean.

    How Germs Spread¶

    How germs spread describes the different paths germs use to travel from one place or person to another. Understanding these paths helps explain why specific hygiene habits work — each habit blocks one of the paths germs use to spread.

    Germs commonly spread through:

    1. Air — when someone coughs or sneezes without covering their mouth, germs travel through the air.
    2. Food — germs can be on food that was not washed, cooked, or stored safely.
    3. Water — untreated or unsafe water can carry germs.
    4. Personal contact — touching another person, shaking hands, or touching a surface someone else touched can pass germs along.

    Comparing these four paths side by side helps show that each one calls for a different prevention habit:

    Path Germs Spread Example Prevention Habit
    Air Uncovered cough or sneeze Cover mouth with elbow, wear a mask if sick
    Food Unwashed or undercooked food Wash produce, cook food fully, store safely
    Water Unsafe drinking water Only drink water a trusted adult says is safe
    Personal contact Shaking hands, touching shared objects Wash hands, avoid touching your face

    You've Got This!

    It might feel like germs are everywhere and impossible to stop, but you already know four simple habits that block every path. You've got this!

    Illness Prevention Practice¶

    Illness prevention practice brings together everything from this chapter into daily habits that keep the body, home, and community healthy. Prevention practices include personal hygiene, keeping shared environments clean, and cultural traditions that support wellness.

    Everyday illness prevention practices include:

    • Washing hands with soap and water before eating and after using the bathroom.
    • Covering coughs and sneezes with an elbow, not hands.
    • Keeping shared spaces, like classroom tables, clean.
    • Getting enough sleep and physical activity so the body can fight off germs.
    • Following cultural or family health traditions, such as specific foods, rest practices, or remedies used to support wellness.

    Illness prevention practice connects directly back to how germs spread: each habit above blocks one or more of the four paths germs use to travel from person to person.

    Quick Check for Read-Aloud Time — Click to expand

    Ask the class: "Your friend sneezes into their hands and then wants to shake your hand. What germ-spread path is this, and what illness prevention practice would help?" Then reveal: "This is the personal contact path. Washing hands (yours and your friend's) and coughing into an elbow instead of hands are both illness prevention practices that would help."

    Chapter Complete!

    Wonderful work, friend! You now know how to protect your body from environmental risks, why physical activity — including games and dances from many cultures — keeps you healthy, and how germs spread and can be prevented. Healthy choices, happy you!

    Wrap-Up for Teachers¶

    By the end of this chapter, most Grade 2 students should be able to describe at least one environmental health risk and a matching protection step, name a form of physical activity (including a cultural example), and explain at least one way germs spread and one way to prevent illness. The MicroSims in this chapter move from an Apply-level match-the-protection activity, to Understand-level body-benefit and cultural-activity explorations, to an Analyze-level germ-path flowchart — matching the Grade 2 emphasis on comparison and cause-effect explanation. Consider pairing this chapter with a classroom discussion inviting students to share a physical activity tradition from their own family or community before moving on to Chapter 5.
