    Foundations of Health and Safe Habits¶

    Summary¶

    This opening chapter introduces the big idea of health and six root ideas that later chapters build on: trusted adults, asking permission, safe behavior, influences on our choices, and communication. Students meet these words for the first time so they can recognize them throughout the rest of Grade 1.

    Concepts Covered¶

    1. Health
    2. Trusted Adult
    3. Permission
    4. Safe Behavior
    5. Influence
    6. Communication

    Prerequisites¶

    This is the first chapter in the Grade 1 band. It assumes only the Kindergarten bridging concepts described in the course description: that a trusted adult is someone at home, school, or in the community you can ask for help, and that simple safety rules exist at home, school, and in the community.

    Teacher Notes: How to Use This Chapter¶

    This chapter is written for you, the teacher, to read aloud, narrate, and guide from. Grade 1 students are beginning independent readers, but comprehension still depends on your read-aloud support. Use the text below as a script while your class explores the pictures and MicroSims. Every interactive element is designed for a child to click, drag, or point at while you talk them through it.

    Hi! I'm Scout.

    Welcome to Grade 1 Health Education! I'm Scout, a friendly dog who loves helping kids make healthy choices. I'll be popping up all through this book, but I don't show up just anywhere — I have exactly six jobs, and you can tell which one I'm doing by my pose:

    1. Welcome you at the start of every chapter — that's what I'm doing right now.
    2. Help you think about a big idea, using my thinking pose.
    3. Give you a tip — a small, helpful trick to remember something.
    4. Warn you gently when something needs extra care or a grown-up's help.
    5. Encourage you when an idea feels new or a little tricky.
    6. Celebrate with you when you've learned something great.

    That's it — six jobs, six poses. If I'm not doing one of those six things, I'm not in the chapter. Let's think it through together, friend. Healthy choices, happy you!

    What Is Health?¶

    Health means how well a person's body and mind are working and feeling. A first grader who is healthy has energy to run, play, and learn. Health also includes feelings — a healthy mind can name how it feels and knows it is okay to talk about those feelings. Health is not just "not being sick" — it also means eating well, moving your body, resting enough, staying safe, and having people you trust to help you.

    Talking points for read-aloud:

    • Ask the class: "What does your body and mind need every day to feel your best?"
    • Guide answers toward food, water, sleep, movement, feelings, and safety.
    • Explain that this whole book is really about health, and today's six words will show up again and again.

    Big Idea

    Health is bigger than just your body. It's your body, your feelings, and the people who help keep you safe — all working together!

    The following table previews the six root ideas in this chapter, in the order the class will meet them:

    Word One-Sentence Meaning
    Health How well your body and mind are working and feeling
    Trusted Adult A grown-up at home, school, or in your community you can go to for help
    Permission Saying yes before someone does something, like using an item or sharing information
    Safe Behavior An action that protects you and others from getting hurt
    Influence Someone or something that affects the choices you make
    Communication Sharing your thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs with someone else

    Meeting a Trusted Adult¶

    A trusted adult is a grown-up at home, school, or in your community who you can go to for help — someone who listens, keeps you safe, and helps you solve a problem. Grade 1 students met this idea in Kindergarten. This year, students explain why a person counts as a trusted adult, not just point to one.

    A trusted adult is usually someone who:

    1. Listens carefully when you talk to them.
    2. Helps you when something feels wrong, confusing, or scary.
    3. Keeps you safe and does not ask you to keep a secret that feels uncomfortable.
    4. Is available to you at home, at school, or in your neighborhood.

    Every child's list of trusted adults may look different — a parent, a grandparent, a teacher, a school nurse, or a neighbor could all count, as long as they meet the qualities above.

    Helpful Tip

    A good trick: think of three grown-ups you could go to if you needed help right now. If you can name three, you already know how to find a trusted adult!

    Implementation notes: Use p5.js. Keep all scenes calm, non-graphic, and age-appropriate — the "not trusted adult" examples should feel mildly "off" rather than scary. Captions large enough for read-aloud.

    Asking Permission¶

    Permission means saying yes before someone does something — like using someone else's belongings, going somewhere new, or sharing a photo or piece of information about someone. Asking permission shows respect for other people and the rules that keep everyone safe. This year, students learn that permission applies to technology too: before using a device or sharing information, a child should ask a trusted adult first.

    Everyday moments where asking permission matters:

    • Asking a parent before downloading a game or app.
    • Asking before taking or sharing a picture of a friend.
    • Asking before borrowing a toy, book, or supply that belongs to someone else.
    • Asking a trusted adult before going somewhere that is not the usual routine.

    Asking first is never rude — it's respectful. Permission protects you, and it protects other people too.

    Choosing Safe Behavior¶

    Safe behavior is an action that protects you and others from getting hurt. The opposite, unsafe behavior, is an action that could cause harm. Safe behavior looks a little different at home, at school, and in the community, but the goal is always the same: keep bodies safe and know when to ask a trusted adult for help.

    Grade 1 students are also learning that keeping their own body safe includes the right to say "no" to touch that feels wrong or unwanted, and to tell a trusted adult right away if that happens.

    Your Body Belongs to You

    If a touch ever feels wrong or makes you uncomfortable, it is always okay to say "no" — loudly and clearly. Then tell a trusted adult right away, even if the person said to keep it a secret.

    Examples of safe behavior in three settings:

    Setting Safe Behavior Example
    Home Buckling a seatbelt, staying away from the stove without an adult nearby
    School Walking in the hallway, lining up during a fire drill
    Community Holding a grown-up's hand near streets, wearing a helmet while biking

    Visual elements: - 10 paired scenes across home, school, and community settings, each clearly showing one safe and one unsafe version of a similar situation

    Interactive controls: - Drag-and-drop or click-to-select: place the scene in the correct bin - Button: "Next Scene" - Button: "Reset"

    Default parameters: - First scene: child holding an adult's hand to cross the street (clearly safe, to build confidence)

    Behavior: - Correct placement: bin glows green or orange to match, gentle chime, one-sentence explanation appears - Incorrect placement: gentle prompt, "Look again — is anyone in danger of getting hurt here?" - Every 3rd scene, an extra prompt asks: "If this were unsafe, who could you tell?" with the answer always modeling "a trusted adult"

    Instructional Rationale: Distinguishing safe from unsafe behavior across multiple settings is an Analyze-level task — it requires comparing visual cues rather than simple recall. Tying the unsafe examples back to "tell a trusted adult" reinforces the connection between Safe Behavior and Trusted Adult introduced earlier in this chapter.

    Implementation notes: Use p5.js. Keep illustrations calm and non-graphic; no scene should depict actual injury. Maintain a plainly sincere tone in all captions, per the project's safety-content voice rule (no puns).

    That's a lot of new words in one chapter! It's okay if they feel new right now — we'll practice them together all year. You've got this!

    Noticing Influence¶

    An influence is a person or thing that affects the choices you make. Influences can be positive, helping you make healthy choices, or negative, pulling you toward choices that are not as healthy. Friends, family, TV shows, and advertisements can all be influences. Grade 1 students begin telling the difference between a positive influence and a negative influence in simple, everyday examples.

    A short list of influences students might notice:

    1. A friend who invites you to play outside (often a positive influence).
    2. A commercial that makes candy look like the best snack ever (often a negative influence).
    3. A family member who reminds you to wear a helmet (a positive influence).
    4. A sibling who dares you to skip washing your hands (a negative influence).

    Not every influence is bad, and not every influence is good — the important part is noticing it, and then deciding for yourself what the healthy choice is.

    Communicating Thoughts and Feelings¶

    Communication means sharing your thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs with someone else, using words, signs, or pictures. Healthy communication also means listening when someone else shares theirs. Grade 1 students practice naming a feeling out loud and telling another person what they need — a skill they will use in every chapter of this book, from asking for help to saying "no" to unsafe touch.

    Simple sentence starters that help young students communicate in healthy ways:

    • "I feel ___ because ___."
    • "I need ___ right now."
    • "Can you help me with ___?"
    • "I don't like ___. Please stop."

    When you're not sure what to say, start with "I feel..." Naming your feeling is the first step to getting the help you need.

    Finish the Sentence — Communication Practice MicroSim

    Type: microsim sim-id: communication-sentence-builder
    Library: p5.js
    Status: Specified

    Bloom Taxonomy: Apply (L3) Bloom Taxonomy Verb: demonstrate, practice, use

    Learning objective: Students demonstrate communicating thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs in healthy ways by completing simple sentence-starter scenarios with the best available response.

    Canvas layout: - Top area (150px): A short scene showing a child in a situation (e.g., feeling left out at recess, needing help with a zipper, feeling scared during a fire drill) - Middle area (250px): Three large word/phrase tiles the student clicks in order to build a short sentence (e.g., "I feel" + "left out" + "will you play with me?") - Bottom strip (100px): The built sentence displays large on screen; "Next Scene" button

    Visual elements: - 6 scenes cycling one at a time, each with 3 tile choices per blank so students assemble a complete, healthy communication sentence

    Default parameters: - First scene: feeling left out at recess

    Behavior: - Once all blanks are filled with a reasonable combination, the character in the scene smiles and a gentle chime plays - No wrong combinations are truly "incorrect" — the MicroSim accepts multiple valid completions, since communication has more than one healthy phrasing, but flags combinations that are unrelated or unkind with a gentle prompt to try again

    Instructional Rationale: This is an Apply-level (demonstrate/practice) objective, so students build and produce a sentence rather than just recognize one. A tile-based builder scaffolds beginning readers who cannot yet type or write full sentences independently.

    Implementation notes: Use p5.js. Keep tile text short (2-4 words) and large. Teacher reads the finished sentence aloud with the student.

    Bringing It All Together¶

    Health is the big idea this whole book explores. This chapter introduced five ideas that support it: a trusted adult to help you, permission to ask before you act, safe behavior to protect yourself and others, influence to notice what shapes your choices, and communication to share what you think, feel, need, and want. Every later Grade 1 chapter — food, feelings, friendship, healthy habits, personal safety, and asking for help — will use these six words again.

    Wrap-Up for Teachers¶

    By the end of this chapter, most Grade 1 students should be able to identify and briefly explain each of the six root ideas: Health, Trusted Adult, Permission, Safe Behavior, Influence, and Communication. Several MicroSims in this chapter go beyond simple recall into explaining why and distinguishing examples, matching the Grade 1 shift toward "identify and explain why." No multi-step evaluative reasoning is expected yet — that begins in Grade 2-3.

    Chapter Complete!

    You made it through the whole chapter, friend! You now know about health, trusted adults, permission, safe behavior, influence, and communication. Keep these six words in your pocket — we'll use every one of them again soon. Healthy choices, happy you!
