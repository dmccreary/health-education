    Getting Help and Making Healthy Choices¶

    Summary¶

    This closing chapter shows how influences can be positive or negative, how communicating feelings and needs helps people get what they need, and how recognizing when help is needed leads to better health decisions. Students also practice tracking healthy behaviors and asking for help with choices.

    Concepts Covered¶

    1. Positive Influence
    2. Negative Influence
    3. Seeking Help
    4. Communicating Feelings
    5. Communicating Needs
    6. Recognizing Need For Help
    7. Health Decision
    8. Tracking Health Behaviors
    9. Asking For Help With Choices

    Prerequisites¶

    This chapter builds on Influence and Communication from Chapter 1: Foundations of Health and Safe Habits, Feelings from Chapter 3: Understanding and Talking About Feelings, Personal Care Practice and Physical Activity from Chapter 5: Healthy Habits, Activity, and Germs, and Asking A Trusted Adult from Chapter 6: Staying Safe at Home, School, and Online.

    Teacher Notes: How to Use This Chapter¶

    This is the final chapter of the Grade 1 book, and it ties together many of the skills students have practiced all year. It covers how outside influences shape health choices, how to communicate feelings and needs clearly, when and how to ask for help, and how to make and track healthy decisions. None of these ideas require the same careful tone as the personal-safety content in Chapter 6, but they do build directly on it — recognizing when you need help and knowing how to ask for it are the same muscles students practiced when learning to tell a trusted adult. Read each section aloud, pause for the discussion prompts, and let students use the MicroSims described below as guided or independent practice, depending on your classroom's technology access.

    Let's Learn About Getting Help and Making Choices

    Hi, friend! Today we're going to learn about the people and things that influence our choices, how to share our feelings and needs with words, and how to know when it's time to ask for help. These skills will help you make healthy choices for the rest of your life.

    Positive Influence: People and Things That Help You Choose Well¶

    Back in Chapter 1, students learned that an influence is a person, thing, or idea that affects how someone thinks or acts. Now it's time to look more closely at two kinds of influence a Grade 1 student meets every day.

    A positive influence is a person, thing, or idea that helps someone make a healthy choice. A positive influence might be a family member who packs a lunch full of fruits and vegetables, a friend who invites you to play outside, or a coach who reminds the team to drink water. Positive influences make it easier, not harder, to take care of your body and feelings.

    Talking points for read-aloud:

    • Ask the class: "Who is someone in your life who helps you make healthy choices?"
    • Explain that a positive influence isn't always a person — a favorite park, a song that makes you want to dance, or a family rule about bedtime can all be positive influences too.
    • Point out that the same influence (like a friend) can be positive in one moment and negative in another, which is why noticing influences is a skill worth practicing.

    Negative Influence: People and Things That Make Healthy Choices Harder¶

    A negative influence is a person, thing, or idea that makes it harder to make a healthy choice, or that encourages an unhealthy or unsafe one. A negative influence might be an advertisement for sugary snacks, a friend who dares someone to skip washing their hands, or a show that makes staying up too late look exciting.

    Recognizing a negative influence does not mean the person or thing is "bad" — it means noticing that, in this one moment, it is pulling a choice in an unhealthy direction. A Grade 1 student is learning to pause and think, "Is this helping me make a healthy choice, or making it harder?"

    Before the diagram below, let's define one more term: to distinguish means to tell two things apart by noticing what makes them different.

    Communicating Feelings: Using Words to Share How You Feel¶

    You've learned to notice influences on your choices. Next, let's look at a skill that helps with almost every part of health: communicating clearly. Students explored feelings in Chapter 3 — feelings are the emotions we experience, like happy, sad, worried, or excited. Communicating feelings means using words, and sometimes gestures or pictures, to tell another person how you feel inside.

    Communicating feelings matters because other people cannot see what's happening inside you. A trusted adult or friend can only help once they know how you feel. Saying "I feel worried about the spelling test" or "I feel happy that you shared your crayons with me" gives other people the information they need to respond in a caring way.

    Here are simple sentence starters a Grade 1 student can use to communicate feelings:

    • "I feel _ because ___."
    • "Right now I am feeling _____."
    • "That made me feel _____."

    Communicating Needs: Using Words to Ask for What You Need¶

    Closely related to communicating feelings is communicating needs — using words to tell another person what you need, such as help, rest, food, or space. A need is different from a feeling: a feeling is how you feel inside, while a need is something that would help you feel better or stay healthy.

    For example, a student who feels tired (a feeling) might communicate the need by saying, "I need to rest for a few minutes" (a need). A student who feels hungry might say, "I need a snack." Communicating needs clearly — instead of just acting upset or frustrated — helps other people understand exactly how to help.

    Here is how feelings and needs work together, using a few common Grade 1 examples:

    Situation Feeling Communicating the Need
    Missed recess because of rain Disappointed "I need to move my body — can we do an indoor activity?"
    Loud, crowded classroom Overwhelmed "I need a quiet moment, please."
    Difficult math problem Frustrated "I need help with this problem."
    Left out of a game Sad "I need a friend to play with."

    You now know two important communication skills: sharing feelings and sharing needs. Sometimes, though, sharing a feeling or a need isn't quite enough on its own — sometimes what's needed is help from another person. Let's look at that next.

    Seeking Help: Reaching Out When You Need Support¶

    Seeking help means reaching out to another person — often a trusted adult — when you need support with a problem, a task, or a feeling. Students already practiced asking a trusted adult in Chapter 6 in the context of safety. Seeking help is the broader skill underneath that: knowing that it is always okay, and often the smart choice, to ask for support rather than struggling alone.

    Seeking help can happen for all kinds of reasons — not just emergencies. A student might seek help to open a stubborn juice box, to understand a confusing homework question, to find a bandage for a scraped knee, or to talk through a disagreement with a friend. There is no situation too small to ask for help with, and there is no situation so big that asking for help is the wrong choice.

    Helpful Tip

    Asking for help is not a sign of weakness — it's a smart, healthy habit. The people who care about you want to know when you need support.

    Recognizing Need For Help: Noticing When It's Time to Ask¶

    Before a student can seek help, they need a skill that comes first: recognizing need for help, which means noticing the signs that a situation calls for support from someone else. This might mean noticing that a task feels too hard to finish alone, that a feeling is too big to handle by yourself, or that a friend seems to be struggling and could use a helper too.

    Recognizing the need for help is like the "uh-oh feeling" students learned about in Chapter 6, but broader: it's the moment of noticing "I can't quite do this by myself" or "This doesn't feel right," and understanding that the very next step is to seek help.

    Here are common signs that it's time to ask for help:

    1. A task keeps feeling too hard, even after trying more than once.
    2. A feeling is very big — like very sad, very worried, or very angry — and it isn't going away.
    3. Something feels unsafe, confusing, or wrong.
    4. A friend or classmate seems to need support too.

    Health Decision: Choosing an Action That Supports Your Wellbeing¶

    All the skills covered so far — noticing influences, communicating feelings and needs, recognizing when to ask for help, and seeking that help — come together in one important idea: the health decision. A health decision is a choice a person makes that affects their health or safety, such as choosing a snack, deciding how to spend free time, or deciding whether to tell a trusted adult about a problem.

    Some health decisions are easy and can be made alone, like choosing water instead of soda. Other health decisions are harder and benefit from asking a trusted adult for support — especially when a positive or negative influence is involved, or when the student isn't sure what to do.

    Let's connect this to something students already know: a positive influence made a health decision easier, while a negative influence made it harder. A student who recognizes a negative influence (like a friend daring them to skip handwashing) can use that recognition to make a healthier decision (washing their hands anyway, and telling a trusted adult if the dare continues).

    Big Idea

    A health decision is a choice that affects your health or safety. Notice the influence, think it through, and remember — asking a trusted adult for help is always part of making a good choice.

    Tracking Health Behaviors: Watching Your Healthy Habits Over Time¶

    Making one good health decision is a great start, but health also grows from tracking health behaviors — watching and recording healthy practices over time, like brushing teeth, being active, or eating a variety of foods. Tracking connects directly to personal care practice and physical activity from Chapter 5: those are the habits, and tracking is how a student notices whether the habits are actually happening regularly.

    Tracking doesn't need to be complicated. A Grade 1 student might use a simple chart with a box to check off each day they brush their teeth, drink water, or play actively outside. Seeing the checked boxes build up over a week helps a student feel proud of their healthy habits and notice patterns, like which days are harder to be active.

    Here are simple ways a Grade 1 student can track health behaviors:

    • A weekly chart with a box to check for each healthy habit completed
    • Stickers placed on a calendar for each day a habit happens
    • A short conversation with a family member each night about the day's healthy choices

    Asking For Help With Choices: Bringing It All Together¶

    The final concept in this chapter — and in the whole Grade 1 book — is asking for help with choices: reaching out to a trusted adult or another trusted person specifically to help think through a health decision. This brings together everything from this chapter: recognizing when help is needed, and using that recognition to make a better health decision.

    Asking for help with a choice does not mean someone else makes the decision instead of the student. It means the student shares the situation, listens to ideas, and then makes a more informed choice. For example, a student unsure whether a snack is a healthy choice might ask, "Can you help me decide what to eat before my game?" A trusted adult can offer information the student didn't have, making the final decision easier and healthier.

    Here are simple phrases a Grade 1 student can use to ask for help with a choice:

    1. "I'm not sure what to do — can you help me think about it?"
    2. "Can you help me decide between these two things?"
    3. "What would you do if you were me?"

    You Can Do This

    Asking for help with a hard choice can feel a little scary at first — that's okay. The more you practice asking, the easier and more natural it becomes, and you'll always have people ready to help you think it through.

    Wrap-Up for Teachers¶

    This chapter, and the Grade 1 book, close by connecting nine ideas into one habit of mind: notice the influence, communicate the feeling or need, recognize when help is needed, seek that help, and use it to make and track a healthy decision. By the end of this chapter, most Grade 1 students should be able to tell a positive influence from a negative one, use a simple sentence to communicate a feeling or need, name a sign that it's time to ask for help, and describe at least one way to track a healthy habit. These are lifelong skills — please continue to revisit them in short check-ins throughout Grade 1 and beyond, connecting them back to the food, feelings, friendship, healthy habits, and safety topics from earlier chapters.
