    Friendship, Uniqueness, and Belonging¶

    Summary¶

    Students examine what makes a peer relationship healthy and how kindness, unkindness, and valuing each person's uniqueness shape caring relationships and a sense of belonging. The chapter ties these ideas together by showing how kindness helps everyone feel like they belong.

    Concepts Covered¶

    Prerequisites¶

    This chapter builds on Health from Chapter 1: Foundations of Health and Safe Habits and on Feelings from Chapter 3: Understanding and Talking About Feelings.

    Teacher Notes: How to Use This Chapter¶

    This chapter is written for you, the teacher, to read aloud and guide from. Grade 1 students are beginning independent readers, so use the text below as a script while your class explores the pictures and MicroSims together. This chapter moves from Kindergarten's simple "be a good friend" idea toward identifying and explaining why — students should be able to name traits of a healthy peer relationship, explain how kindness and unkindness change how people feel, and describe how valuing everyone's uniqueness helps a whole classroom feel like it belongs together.

    Let's Talk About Friends and Belonging!

    Hi again, friend! Today we're exploring what makes a good friend, why kindness matters so much, and how every single person — including you — makes our class a better place just by being who they are. Let's think it through together. Healthy choices, happy you!

    What Is a Peer Relationship?¶

    A peer is someone close to your own age — a classmate, a neighbor kid, a teammate. A peer relationship is the connection you have with a peer: how you play together, talk together, and treat each other over time. Grade 1 students have many peer relationships already, at school, on a team, or in the neighborhood, even if they've never used that exact word before.

    Talking points for read-aloud:

    • Ask the class: "Who is a peer of yours? Someone close to your age that you spend time with?"
    • Explain that a peer relationship can be a friendship, but not every peer is automatically a "best friend" — and that's okay.
    • Remind students that peer relationships take practice, just like any other skill.

    Some peer relationships feel easy and comfortable, and some take more work. Both kinds are normal, and learning what makes a relationship healthy helps a Grade 1 student build stronger friendships all year long.

    Healthy Peer Traits: What Makes a Relationship Healthy?¶

    Healthy peer traits are the habits and qualities that make a peer relationship feel safe, fair, and good for both people. A healthy peer relationship doesn't mean two people never disagree — it means they treat each other well even when they do.

    Here are traits of a healthy peer relationship:

    • Listening when the other person talks
    • Taking turns and sharing fairly
    • Telling the truth, even about mistakes
    • Including others instead of leaving them out
    • Saying sorry and meaning it after a mistake
    • Cheering for the other person's success

    Big Idea

    A healthy peer relationship isn't about never disagreeing — it's about how you treat each other, even when you do! Fair, honest, and caring — that's what makes a friendship strong.

    Kindness: Small Actions That Help Others Feel Good¶

    Kindness means doing or saying something that helps another person feel good, safe, or cared for — on purpose. Kindness can be small, like sharing a crayon, or bigger, like inviting a lonely classmate to play. Kindness doesn't have to cost anything or take a long time; often the smallest kind actions matter the most.

    Examples of kindness a Grade 1 student can practice every day:

    1. Saying "You can play with us!"
    2. Helping a classmate pick up dropped papers
    3. Giving a genuine compliment
    4. Sharing supplies without being asked
    5. Checking on someone who looks sad or left out

    Kindness doesn't need a special occasion — a simple "Do you want to play?" can turn someone's whole day around.

    Unkindness: How Hurtful Actions Affect Others¶

    Unkindness means doing or saying something that hurts, excludes, or embarrasses another person, whether on purpose or without thinking it through. Unkindness can look like teasing, leaving someone out on purpose, laughing at a mistake, or grabbing something without asking. Even small unkind moments can make a person feel sad, unsafe, or like they don't belong.

    It's important for a Grade 1 student to understand that everyone makes mistakes sometimes, and noticing an unkind moment is a chance to say sorry and choose differently next time — not a reason to feel like a "bad person" forever.

    Common examples of unkindness, and a kinder choice instead:

    Unkind Action How It Feels A Kinder Choice
    Leaving someone out on purpose Lonely, sad "Come join us!"
    Laughing at someone's mistake Embarrassed "That's okay, everyone makes mistakes."
    Grabbing a toy without asking Angry, disrespected "Can I have a turn when you're done?"
    Teasing about how someone looks or talks Hurt, self-conscious Notice and appreciate the difference instead
    Whispering about someone nearby Excluded, suspicious Invite them into the conversation

    Common Mistake

    Sometimes unkindness happens without someone meaning to be mean — like forgetting to invite a classmate. Noticing it and fixing it (saying sorry, inviting them next time) is what matters most!

    You've now explored what makes a peer relationship healthy, and how kindness and unkindness change the way people feel. Next, let's talk about what makes each of us wonderfully different — and why that's something to celebrate.

    Uniqueness: Every Person Is One of a Kind¶

    Uniqueness means the special combination of traits, interests, abilities, family background, and appearance that makes each person different from everyone else. No two people are exactly alike — not even twins! One classmate might use a wheelchair, another might speak two languages at home, another might have curly red hair, and another might love bugs more than anything else in the world. All of these differences are simply part of what makes each person who they are.

    Talking points for read-aloud:

    • Ask the class: "What is something unique about you? A talent, a family tradition, something you love?"
    • Emphasize that uniqueness includes things we can see (appearance, ability) and things we can't see right away (family type, interests, how someone learns best).
    • Keep the tone warm and matter-of-fact — uniqueness is simply a fact about being human, never a reason to single someone out.

    Examples of the many kinds of uniqueness in any classroom:

    • Different physical abilities (some students use glasses, hearing aids, wheelchairs, or other tools)
    • Different family types (two parents, one parent, grandparents, foster families, blended families)
    • Different backgrounds, languages, and cultural traditions
    • Different interests, talents, and ways of learning
    • Different appearances — hair, skin color, height, and more

    Valuing Uniqueness: Why Differences Make Us Stronger¶

    Valuing uniqueness means noticing and appreciating what makes each person different, instead of expecting everyone to be the same. When a classroom values uniqueness, it means no one has to hide the things that make them who they are in order to fit in. Valuing uniqueness strengthens caring relationships — connections between people built on trust, respect, and support — because people feel safe being their true selves with each other. A classroom full of people who are exactly the same would be pretty boring — differences are what make a class interesting, and valuing them helps everyone feel safe being themselves.

    Everyone Belongs Just as They Are

    You never have to change who you are to be liked. Your family, your abilities, your interests, and your looks are all part of what makes you a wonderful friend to have.

    Belonging: Feeling Like You Fit In and Matter¶

    Belonging is the feeling of being welcomed, included, and valued as part of a group — knowing that you fit in and matter, just as you are. A student who belongs in a classroom feels comfortable raising a hand, sitting with others at lunch, and joining a game at recess, without worrying about being left out or made fun of.

    Belonging grows out of caring relationships — the ongoing connections between people built on trust, respect, and support. A single caring relationship, even with just one friend or one caring adult, can help a student feel like they belong, even in a big, busy classroom.

    Signs that someone feels a sense of belonging:

    • Joining in activities without hesitating
    • Sharing ideas out loud without fear of being laughed at
    • Sitting with others at lunch or on the bus
    • Feeling comfortable asking for help
    • Smiling, relaxed body language around peers

    Kindness and Belonging: Bringing It All Together¶

    Kindness and belonging are deeply connected: every act of kindness is a small brick that helps build a classroom where everyone feels like they belong. When students practice healthy peer traits, choose kindness over unkindness, and value each other's uniqueness, the whole class becomes a place where every single person feels welcomed, valued, and safe to be themselves.

    Think of it this way: a peer relationship becomes healthier through kindness, unkindness chips away at belonging, and valuing each person's uniqueness is what makes belonging possible for everyone — not just students who are similar to each other. Caring relationships are the glue that holds all of these ideas together.

    Helpful Tip

    Next time you're not sure what to do, ask yourself: "Will this help someone feel like they belong?" If the answer is yes, that's a kind choice!

    Wrap-Up for Teachers¶

    This chapter connected nine ideas: what a peer relationship is, the healthy traits that strengthen one, how kindness and unkindness change the way people feel, what uniqueness means, why valuing uniqueness matters, what belonging feels like, what caring relationships are, and how kindness and belonging work together. By the end of this chapter, most Grade 1 students should be able to describe at least two healthy peer traits, explain how kindness (or unkindness) affects a relationship, name something unique about themselves and a classmate, and explain why valuing everyone's uniqueness helps the whole class feel like they belong. Keep discussion of uniqueness warm and matter-of-fact; no student's difference should ever be framed as unusual in a negative sense.

    Chapter Complete!

    Wonderful work, friend! You've learned what makes a peer relationship healthy, how kindness helps everyone feel like they belong, and why every person's uniqueness is something worth celebrating. Healthy choices, happy you!
