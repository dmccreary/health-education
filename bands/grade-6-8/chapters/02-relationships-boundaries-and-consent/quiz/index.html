    Quiz: Relationships, Boundaries, and Consent¶

    Test your understanding of relationship structures, healthy and unhealthy patterns, boundary-setting, and consent with these review questions.

    1. Which of the following is one of the four defining features of consent described in this chapter?¶

    1. Permanent — once given, consent cannot be withdrawn
    2. Assumed — silence counts as agreement unless someone objects
    3. One-time — a single agreement covers all future related situations
    4. Reversible — a person can change their mind, even partway through, and that change must be respected immediately
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter lists reversibility as one of consent's four features, along with being freely given, specific, and informed. It specifically notes that silence or not saying "no" is not the same as saying "yes," which rules out the "assumed" option.

    Concept Tested: Consent

    2. What is coercion, as defined in this chapter?¶

    1. Pressuring, guilting, manipulating, or threatening someone into agreeing to something they would not otherwise choose
    2. Any disagreement between two people in a relationship
    3. A one-time argument that gets resolved through compromise
    4. Asking someone directly for something and accepting whatever answer they give
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Coercion replaces genuine willingness with pressure, fear, or exhaustion. A fair request that respects the other person's answer, like option D, is the opposite of coercion, not an example of it.

    Concept Tested: Coercion

    3. What does the chapter say determines whether a relationship structure, such as a family arrangement, is healthy?¶

    1. Whether it matches the most common structure in the student's school
    2. Whether it is a two-parent household
    3. Whether it provides care, safety, and respect, not which specific structure it takes
    4. Whether family members share the same last name
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter explains that no relationship structure is inherently better than another. What matters for health is whether a relationship, family or otherwise, actually provides care, safety, and respect.

    Concept Tested: Relationship Structures

    4. What does "fair conflict" mean as a healthy relationship characteristic?¶

    1. A healthy relationship never has any disagreements at all
    2. Disagreements happen but get resolved through listening and compromise rather than control or punishment
    3. Conflicts are healthy only if both people raise their voices equally
    4. Fair conflict means avoiding difficult topics entirely
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter is explicit that healthy relationships are not conflict-free; what makes conflict "fair" is that it gets resolved through listening and compromise instead of control or punishment, and that both people still feel safe afterward.

    Concept Tested: Healthy Relationship Characteristics

    5. A sibling snaps at another sibling once, then apologizes, and the relationship continues as normal. According to the chapter, how should this be understood?¶

    1. As a single, non-pattern moment, since one hard moment doesn't automatically make a relationship unhealthy
    2. As proof the relationship is unhealthy, since any hostile behavior counts as unhealthy
    3. As a healthy characteristic, since snapping shows honesty
    4. As a form of coercion, since raising one's voice is a threat
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter emphasizes that what matters is the pattern, not a single moment. A one-time lapse that gets acknowledged and repaired is different from control, disrespect, or manipulation that shows up repeatedly and goes unaddressed.

    Concept Tested: Unhealthy Relationship Characteristics

    6. How do collectivist cultural orientations tend to frame a personal boundary, compared to individualist orientations?¶

    1. As something that should never be discussed when family is present
    2. As identical to individualist orientations, with no real difference
    3. As primarily about protecting one's own individual autonomy above all else
    4. Within a web of relationships and obligations, considering how the boundary affects family or community alongside the individual
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Collectivist orientations, common in many Indigenous, Asian, Latin American, and African traditions, frame boundaries with attention to how they affect family or community, while individualist orientations tend to frame a boundary mainly as protecting one's own autonomy.

    Concept Tested: Cultural Values Of Respect And Balance

    7. A classmate says, "If you were really my friend, you'd let me copy your homework." Which coercion tactic is this, and how should it be recognized?¶

    1. Repeated pressure — asking the same thing many times until the person agrees
    2. Guilt-tripping — framing the request so that declining feels like proof of not caring
    3. A fair request that deserves a lengthy explanation in response
    4. Exploiting power differences based on age or popularity
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This statement frames saying no as evidence of not being a real friend, which is the defining pattern of guilt-tripping. Recognizing this tactic helps the classmate's friend see it as pressure rather than a fair, respectful request.

    Concept Tested: Coercion

    8. Using the three-part boundary-setting structure from this chapter, which response best sets a clear boundary when a friend keeps asking to see private messages?¶

    1. Changing the subject without saying anything about the request
    2. Sharing just one message to make the friend stop asking
    3. "I'm not comfortable sharing that. If you ask again, my answer is still no."
    4. Apologizing at length for not wanting to share, then sharing anyway
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. This response names the limit clearly, states it directly, and signals willingness to restate it if needed — the three parts of effective boundary-setting described in the chapter. It does not require an apology or lengthy justification.

    Concept Tested: Personal Boundary-Setting

    9. In an early dating relationship, one partner asks, "Are you still comfortable with this plan?" before continuing. What does this represent?¶

    1. Consent functioning as an ongoing, everyday habit in a healthy relationship
    2. Unnecessary caution, since an earlier yes should cover all future moments
    3. A sign the relationship lacks trust
    4. A formal, one-time event rather than routine behavior
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter describes consent as an ongoing habit rather than a one-time checkbox. Checking in, instead of assuming a past yes covers every future moment, is exactly what makes consent function naturally inside a healthy relationship.

    Concept Tested: Consent In Healthy Relationships

    10. Two friends are texting. One asks the other to hang out; the second hesitates and says "maybe not today." In Scenario 1, the first friend replies "okay, no worries" and drops it. In Scenario 2, the first friend keeps asking until the second friend gives in. What does comparing these two scenarios show?¶

    1. Both scenarios reflect consent, since the same friend eventually got an answer
    2. Scenario 1 reflects consent because the answer is accepted without argument; Scenario 2 reflects coercion because the "no" is treated as a problem to solve
    3. Both scenarios reflect coercion, since asking a second time is always pressure
    4. Scenario 2 is healthier because persistence shows how much the friend cares
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's comparison of consent and coercion turns on how a "no" or hesitation is handled: consent accepts the answer and stays open to it, while coercion treats "no" as something to overcome through continued pressure until the person gives in.

    Concept Tested: Consent Versus Coercion
