    Quiz: Health Promotion and Behavior Change¶

    Test your understanding of the behavior-change cycle, health promotion across levels, and designing fact-based health messages with these review questions.

    1. What are the four steps of the behavior-change cycle described in the chapter?¶

    1. Define, Explore, Consider, Identify
    2. Formulate, Justify, Tailor, Evaluate
    3. Individual, Interpersonal, Community, Policy
    4. Evaluate supports, evaluate barriers, adapt the behavior, evaluate impact
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter's behavior-change cycle walks through evaluating supports, evaluating barriers, adapting the behavior, and evaluating impact, illustrated through Jordan's active-commute example, with the cycle looping back to evaluating supports after each round.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Supports For Health Practices

    2. Why does the chapter describe weather as sometimes a barrier and sometimes a support for Jordan's active commute?¶

    1. Weather is always classified as a barrier, with no exceptions
    2. The same factor can shift between the support and barrier column depending on conditions, which is why evaluating supports and barriers is an ongoing process rather than a one-time list
    3. Weather has no real effect on whether someone can walk or bike to school
    4. Weather should never be considered when evaluating a health practice
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explicitly notes that weather is a barrier on rainy days but a non-issue, even a support, on clear days, illustrating that evaluating supports and barriers means noticing which conditions shift a factor from one column to the other, so a plan can shift with it.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Barriers To Health Practices

    3. How does the chapter distinguish "adapting" a health practice from simply quitting it or forcing an unworkable plan to continue unchanged?¶

    1. Adapting treats the original goal as fixed while treating the specific method as flexible, modifying the plan to lean on supports and route around barriers rather than abandoning the goal or forcing a rigid method
    2. Adapting means changing the underlying goal itself whenever a barrier appears
    3. Adapting is identical to quitting, just described with more positive language
    4. Adapting means ignoring barriers entirely and hoping they resolve on their own
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter explains that adapting treats the original goal, such as active commuting, as fixed while treating the specific method as flexible. Jordan didn't abandon the goal when barriers appeared; the plan adapted by changing timing, method, people involved, or environment while keeping the underlying health goal intact.

    Concept Tested: Adapting Health Behaviors

    4. Jordan notices a safe sidewalk for most of the route, a bike, a nearby friend who also wants to walk, and a school policy allowing early building access. According to the chapter, what category do these all belong to?¶

    1. Barriers that will need to be worked around
    2. Justification evidence for a health message
    3. Supports — the people, resources, environments, and habits already working in Jordan's favor
    4. Effectiveness evaluation criteria
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter's support-type table categorizes exactly these examples, a friend (person), a sidewalk and bike (place/resource), the early-access policy (policy/environment), and an existing habit, as supports: resources already present and working in Jordan's favor, even though supports are easy to overlook precisely because they're already there.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Supports For Health Practices

    5. The same justified fact about nicotine's effect on the developing brain needs to reach both younger students and a school board. According to the chapter, what should stay the same and what should change?¶

    1. Both the facts and the delivery format should be completely rewritten for each audience, including changing what is actually true
    2. Nothing should change between audiences, since tailoring is unnecessary once a message is justified
    3. Only the channel should ever change; the language and tone must stay identical for every audience
    4. The underlying evidence and claim should stay intact, while tone, vocabulary, channel, and which facts to lead with are adjusted to fit each specific audience's concerns and reading level
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter is explicit that tailoring is not the same as changing the facts to fit what an audience wants to hear — that would break the justification requirement. It means choosing which true, well-supported facts and which delivery format will actually land with a specific audience, while the underlying evidence and claim remain intact.

    Concept Tested: Tailoring Messages To Audiences

    6. Why does the chapter argue that a campaign aimed only at individual knowledge ("vaping is bad for you"), without any interpersonal, community, environmental, or policy support, is likely to fail?¶

    1. Individual-level messaging is always the single most effective lever and never needs support from other levels
    2. It asks individuals to resist alone what the whole surrounding system may still be making easy or normal, since effective health promotion usually stacks levers across multiple levels rather than relying on just one
    3. Interpersonal and community levels have no real influence on health behavior
    4. Policy-level change is the only level that ever matters for health promotion
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that a campaign relying only on individual-level knowledge asks individuals to resist alone what the whole surrounding system may still be making easy or normal. Effective health promotion usually stacks levers across the individual, interpersonal, community, environmental, and policy levels, since they reinforce each other.

    Concept Tested: Health Promotion Factors Across Levels

    7. A health campaign poster goes viral and gets shared thousands of times, but a follow-up survey shows students' actual vaping rates and attitudes haven't changed at all. Was this an effective health promotion effort?¶

    1. No, because attention is not the same as effectiveness, and evaluating effectiveness requires checking the actual outcome the message was meant to produce, not just its reach
    2. Yes, because a large number of shares always indicates a successful health promotion effort
    3. Yes, because getting attention is the only criterion that matters for evaluating a health message
    4. The question cannot be evaluated without knowing exactly who shared the poster
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter's own quick-check example addresses this exact scenario, concluding that a message can be widely seen and still fail to shift knowledge, attitude, or behavior. Evaluating effectiveness means looking past whether a message merely got attention to whether it changed the outcome it was meant to produce.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Health Promotion Effectiveness

    8. Comparing "Vaping is really dangerous for teens" to "The developing adolescent brain continues forming reward-system connections until the mid-twenties, and nicotine exposure during this window is associated with a higher risk of future addiction, a mechanism documented by public health and medical research," which is the stronger justification, and why?¶

    1. Neither statement can function as justification for a health message
    2. Both statements are equally strong, since they express the same underlying concern
    3. The second statement, because it cites a specific, checkable mechanism and evidence rather than a general impression, which the chapter identifies as the difference between weak and strong justification
    4. The first statement, because shorter claims are always more persuasive to a skeptical audience
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter contrasts a weak justification ("Vaping is really dangerous for teens") with a strong one that cites a specific, checkable mechanism and evidence base. Justifying a message with facts means citing a specific, verifiable data point or mechanism, not just a general impression, since audiences often judge a message by whether its claims hold up.

    Concept Tested: Justifying Health Messages With Facts

    9. Over four weeks, Jordan's days-walked count dips in week 3 due to rain, while morning stress and resting heart rate both continue improving. Evaluating this data using the chapter's approach, what is the strongest conclusion?¶

    1. The adapted plan was a complete failure since one measure temporarily dipped
    2. Only the days-walked measure should be considered; the other two measures are irrelevant
    3. A single dip in any measure automatically means the underlying health goal should be abandoned
    4. Impact evaluation should look at more than one kind of outcome, since a practice can succeed on one dimension (stress, heart rate) and stall on another (days walked) due to an identifiable cause like a rainy week
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter explicitly states that impact evaluation should look at more than one kind of outcome, because a practice can succeed on one dimension while stalling on another. Jordan's data shows exactly this pattern, with a rain-related dip in one measure alongside continued improvement in stress and heart rate.

    Concept Tested: Evaluating Impact Of Health Behaviors

    10. A student wants to formulate a health message but has only written the vague topic "vaping is bad." Using the chapter's guidance on formulating a message with a genuine call to action, which revision best meets the standard?¶

    1. "Be aware of vaping," since raising general awareness is always a sufficient call to action
    2. "Nicotine exposure during adolescence rewires the brain's reward pathways in ways that make future addiction more likely — talk to a trusted adult before trying any nicotine product, even one a friend says is harmless," since it states a specific, learnable point and a concrete action
    3. "Vaping," since a single-word topic is the clearest possible message
    4. "Some people think vaping might not be great," since a tentative claim is always more persuasive
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter distinguishes a vague topic like "vaping is bad" from a formulated message with real content the audience can learn from and a genuine call to action, giving nearly this exact example: a specific brain-development claim paired with a concrete action ("talk to a trusted adult") rather than a weak instruction like "be aware."
