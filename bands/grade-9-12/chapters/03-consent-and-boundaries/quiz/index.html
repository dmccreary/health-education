    Quiz: Consent and Boundaries¶

    Test your understanding of consent, personal boundaries, and the power and legal frameworks that shape them with these review questions.

    1. Which of the following is one of the five components consent must have, according to the chapter's model?¶

    1. Permanent, meaning it cannot be withdrawn once given
    2. Assumed, meaning it does not need to be stated aloud
    3. Specific, meaning agreeing to one thing does not mean agreeing to another
    4. Silent, meaning the absence of objection counts as agreement
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Specific is one of the five required components: agreeing to one activity, on one occasion, does not extend automatically to a different activity, time, or situation. The other options directly contradict the chapter's model — consent is reversible rather than permanent, must be actively communicated rather than assumed, and requires an enthusiastic yes rather than mere silence.

    Concept Tested: Consent

    2. How does the chapter distinguish compassion from dignity?¶

    1. Dignity and compassion mean exactly the same thing in this framework
    2. Dignity means not violating someone's boundaries; compassion means actively considering their comfort, safety, and right to change their mind
    3. Compassion only applies to family relationships, while dignity applies to strangers
    4. Dignity is optional, while compassion is legally required
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter describes dignity as the passive principle of not violating a person's boundaries, while compassion is the active component of considering what someone is experiencing before and during any interaction. Together they describe a relationship or decision built on respect rather than persuasion or management of another person.

    Concept Tested: Dignity And Compassion For All People

    3. Why does the chapter distinguish "freely given" from "enthusiastic" as two separate requirements of consent, rather than treating them as the same thing?¶

    1. Because freely given only applies to legal contexts, while enthusiastic only applies to relationships
    2. Because the two terms are interchangeable and either one alone is sufficient
    3. Because enthusiastic consent is less important than freely given consent
    4. Because someone could technically avoid pressure yet still respond passively rather than with a genuine, active yes
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Freely given addresses the absence of pressure, manipulation, or threats, while enthusiastic addresses whether the response is an active, genuine yes rather than mere absence of a "no." A response could technically lack outside pressure yet still be passive compliance, which is why both requirements must be checked separately for consent to be complete.

    Concept Tested: Consent

    4. Why does the chapter state that silence and freezing are not the same as consent?¶

    1. Because they are documented, often involuntary responses that can be mistaken for calm acceptance rather than genuine agreement
    2. Because silence is always a sign that a person is lying about their true feelings
    3. Because freezing only occurs in situations involving strangers
    4. Because any quiet response should be interpreted as a clear no rather than requiring further interpretation
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Freezing is a documented, involuntary stress response where a person goes still or compliant when frightened, rather than actively resisting. Both silence and freezing are frequently misread as agreement precisely because they are quiet responses that can resemble calm acceptance, which is why interpreting consent accurately requires checking directly rather than assuming.

    Concept Tested: Communicating And Interpreting Consent

    5. A student says, "I don't want to do that," and a friend responds, "Why not? You have to give me a reason." What does the chapter say about this response?¶

    1. A boundary does not require justification to be valid; "no" is a complete sentence
    2. The friend is correct, since boundaries only count once they are explained
    3. The student's boundary is invalid because it wasn't stated in advance
    4. Boundaries only apply to physical touch, not to this kind of request
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter states directly that a boundary does not require justification to be valid — a boundary does not become more valid by attaching a longer explanation to it. Demanding a reason before respecting a stated limit misunderstands how boundary communication is supposed to work.

    Concept Tested: Personal Boundary Communication

    6. Two people agree to start a physical activity together, and partway through, one person says they want to stop. What is the only response the chapter describes as appropriate?¶

    1. Finishing what was already started, since agreement was given at the beginning
    2. Asking the person one more time to confirm before stopping
    3. Stopping immediately, without protest, guilt, or negotiation
    4. Continuing only if the other person doesn't clearly repeat the request
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Consent can be withdrawn mid-activity, and agreeing to start something is not the same as agreeing to finish it. The chapter states the only appropriate response to withdrawn consent is to stop immediately and without protest, guilt, or negotiation — any other response converts what was consensual into something that no longer is.

    Concept Tested: Withdrawing Consent

    7. A coach who controls an athlete's playing time and college recommendation asks the athlete to keep a personal secret between just the two of them. Based on the chapter's discussion of power dynamics, why is this situation different from a similar request between two peers?¶

    1. It isn't different — power dynamics only matter in romantic relationships
    2. The coach's request is automatically illegal regardless of context
    3. Athletic relationships are exempt from the power-dynamics framework
    4. The coach's authority over the athlete's opportunities can distort the athlete's real ability to say no, even without any explicit threat
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Authority relationships, such as coach and athlete, are named in the chapter as a source of power imbalance because one person can affect the other's opportunities or standing. This can undermine a person's genuine ability to refuse, even if no explicit threat is ever spoken, which is different from a request between people on equal footing.

    Concept Tested: Power Dynamics And Consent

    8. In a described scenario, a person gives a vague, delayed verbal response, avoids eye contact, and seems tense — after being asked repeatedly. According to the chapter's signal comparison, how should this most likely be interpreted?¶

    1. As a genuine yes, since the person eventually gave a verbal response
    2. As likely reluctant compliance or a non-answer, based on the combination of hesitant tone, tense body language, and prior pressure
    3. As clearly informed consent, since a response was given at all
    4. As irrelevant, since only the words spoken matter
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's comparison table links vague or delayed verbal responses, tense body language, hesitant tone, and a context of prior persistence to likely reluctant compliance or a non-answer, rather than a genuine yes. Genuine consent is associated with clear, unprompted, confident responses without preceding pressure.

    Concept Tested: Communicating And Interpreting Consent

    9. Why does the chapter say the law does not simply ask "did the person say yes" when determining whether consent was valid?¶

    1. Because spoken words are always irrelevant to legal questions about consent
    2. Because the law only cares about written consent forms
    3. Because the law examines whether the surrounding situation made a genuinely free yes possible in the first place, since power imbalances can make agreement not truly free even when words are spoken
    4. Because courts are not permitted to consider context in these cases
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter explains that a "yes" spoken under a real or perceived threat to something a person needs or values is not the same as a freely given yes, even if the words are identical. This is why the law examines whether the situation made genuine consent possible, rather than treating spoken agreement alone as sufficient.

    Concept Tested: Law And Consent

    10. Why might a situation involving consent be governed by tribal law rather than, or in addition to, state or federal law?¶

    1. Tribal nations have no legal authority over any matters involving their own members or lands
    2. State law automatically overrides tribal law in every circumstance
    3. Federal law is the only law that has ever applied on tribal lands
    4. Many tribal nations are sovereign governments with their own legal systems, and jurisdiction can depend on location and the people involved
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter explains that many tribal nations are sovereign governments with their own legal systems, and depending on the location, the people involved, and the specific law, a tribal nation's laws and courts may have jurisdiction rather than, or in addition to, state or federal law. This reflects the layered, multi-jurisdictional nature of law described in the chapter rather than a single uniform system.

    Concept Tested: Law And Consent
