    Quiz: Substance Policy and Law¶

    Test your understanding of substance laws, tribal sovereignty, financial costs, and marketing policy with these review questions.

    1. Even in states that have fully legalized recreational cannabis, what remains true about its status under federal law?¶

    1. Cannabis remains classified as a controlled substance under federal law, meaning a behavior can be legal under state law while still technically prohibited federally
    2. Federal law automatically adopts whatever each state decides
    3. States lose all authority to regulate cannabis once federal law changes
    4. Federal law has no jurisdiction over any substance regulated by a state
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter explains that cannabis remains classified as a controlled substance under federal law even in states that have fully legalized it for recreational adult use. This layered federal and state structure means a behavior can be legal under state law and still technically prohibited under federal law.

    Concept Tested: Community Laws On Substance Use

    2. What does "tribal sovereignty" mean in the context of substance use lawmaking?¶

    1. Tribal nations must always follow the exact same substance laws as the surrounding state
    2. Tribal governments can only enforce laws that a state legislature has first approved
    3. A tribal nation's laws come from its own governing authority as a sovereign government, not from a state permitting it to act or the federal government delegating power downward
    4. Tribal sovereignty is a cultural term with no legal or political meaning
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter emphasizes that tribal sovereignty is a legal and political term: tribal nations are sovereign governments with a government-to-government relationship with the federal government, and their laws come from their own governing authority, not from permission granted by a state or the federal government.

    Concept Tested: Tribal Sovereignty In Substance Use Lawmaking

    3. Which four categories does the chapter use to group the financial and social costs of substance use?¶

    1. Marketing costs, zoning costs, licensing costs, and tax costs
    2. Age-restriction costs, possession costs, sale costs, and distribution costs
    3. Federal costs, state costs, local costs, and tribal costs
    4. Healthcare costs, lost productivity, criminal justice costs, and family and community costs
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter organizes financial and social costs of substance use into healthcare costs, lost productivity, criminal justice costs, and family and community costs, noting that these costs are not evenly distributed across communities and are borne by different combinations of individuals, employers, governments, and families.

    Concept Tested: Financial And Social Costs Of Substance Use

    4. A tribal nation legalizes and regulates its own cannabis market on tribal land, even though the surrounding state prohibits cannabis entirely. How should this situation be understood?¶

    1. As a violation of state law that the tribal nation has no authority to commit
    2. As an example of tribal sovereignty, since tribal nations have independent authority to regulate substances on tribal land regardless of the surrounding state's laws
    3. As evidence that state law automatically overrides tribal law in every case
    4. As a temporary arrangement that only exists until the state changes its own law
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter gives this exact example: some tribal nations have legalized and regulate their own cannabis markets on tribal land, sometimes in states where cannabis remains illegal under state law. This reflects tribal sovereignty, the independent authority of tribal nations to regulate substances within their own jurisdiction.

    Concept Tested: Tribal Sovereignty In Substance Use Lawmaking

    5. A vaping advertisement uses bright colors, sweet flavors, and a social-media-native format, while technically complying with all marketing laws. How should this ad be evaluated using the chapter's media-literacy framework?¶

    1. Legal compliance and actual reach to minors are separate questions worth evaluating independently, since research shows certain imagery and flavors can appeal to minors even when an ad is legally targeted at adults
    2. Since the ad is technically legal, it must also be free of any influence on people under the legal age
    3. Marketing choices like color and flavor have no documented connection to youth appeal
    4. The ad cannot be evaluated at all without knowing the exact sales figures it produced
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter explicitly warns against assuming legal marketing is neutral marketing: "is this ad technically legal?" and "is this ad actually reaching only adults?" are two different questions, and marketing research repeatedly finds that certain imagery, flavors, and placement strategies reach and appeal to minors despite legally targeting adults.

    Concept Tested: Substance Marketing And Availability Policy

    6. Why do economists and public health researchers pay close attention to excise taxes on products like cigarettes as a tool for reducing youth substance use?¶

    1. Because excise taxes have no documented effect on purchasing behavior at any age
    2. Because youth purchasing is especially price-sensitive, making higher taxes one of the most consistently documented tools for reducing youth initiation
    3. Because excise tax revenue is never used for prevention or treatment programs
    4. Because taxes only affect adult purchasing decisions, not youth decisions
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that because youth purchasing is especially price-sensitive, higher taxes on products like cigarettes are one of the most consistently documented tools for reducing youth initiation, which is why tax policy appears repeatedly in public health debates about substance use, not only debates about government revenue.

    Concept Tested: Financial And Social Costs Of Substance Use

    7. Research consistently links higher retail density of alcohol and tobacco outlets in a neighborhood to what outcome?¶

    1. No measurable change in substance use or related harm
    2. Lower rates of use, since more competition among retailers reduces prices
    3. Retail density has been shown to have no connection to health outcomes
    4. Higher rates of substance use and related harms in the surrounding neighborhood
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The chapter states that research consistently links higher retail density of alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis outlets to higher rates of use and related harms in the surrounding neighborhood, which is why zoning and licensing decisions about retail availability are treated as a public health policy lever, not just a business question.

    Concept Tested: Substance Marketing And Availability Policy

    8. Historical tobacco marketing that specifically targeted Black communities with menthol cigarette advertising is presented in the chapter as an example of what?¶

    1. A marketing strategy with no lasting effect on any community's health outcomes
    2. An unrelated historical event with no connection to current health disparities
    3. A documented mechanism producing substance-related health disparities, contributing to disproportionately high menthol-cigarette use and related disease rates in targeted communities
    4. A universal marketing approach applied equally to every demographic group
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter names historical menthol cigarette marketing targeted at Black communities as a documented pattern that contributed to disproportionately high menthol-cigarette use and related disease rates in those communities, illustrating how marketing policy is one of the clearest mechanisms producing substance-related health disparities.

    Concept Tested: Substance-Related Health Disparities

    9. Why does the chapter argue that substance-related health disparities should be understood as a policy outcome that can change, rather than a fixed fact about a community?¶

    1. Because disparities are entirely random and unrelated to any identifiable cause
    2. Because disparities never change regardless of any policy adjustment
    3. Because measurable shifts occur when policy changes, such as tightening outlet-density zoning near schools or a tribal nation funding its own culturally grounded treatment program, showing the disparity responds to policy rather than being unavoidable
    4. Because only individual behavior, not policy, determines disparity outcomes
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter gives concrete examples, tightened zoning, closed marketing loopholes, and tribally funded treatment programs, where a disparity measurably shifts in response to a policy change. This responsiveness to policy is exactly why researchers and advocates analyze laws and regulations rather than treating disparities as an unavoidable fact about a community.

    Concept Tested: Substance-Related Health Disparities

    10. A tribal nation regulates alcohol sales on its land more strictly than the surrounding state, citing community-specific public health priorities connected to documented historical harms. How should this policy choice be evaluated?¶

    1. As an example of a state overriding tribal authority
    2. As a legitimate exercise of tribal sovereignty, reflecting a community's own authority to set public health policy based on its specific history and priorities
    3. As a policy with no connection to public health evidence
    4. As a violation of federal alcohol law that tribal nations are not permitted to enact
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter describes this exact scenario as an example of tribal sovereignty in action: some tribal nations regulate or restrict alcohol sales more strictly than the surrounding state, reflecting community-specific public health priorities, including responses to alcohol's documented historical harms in some Indigenous communities.
