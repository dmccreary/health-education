### What Are Feelings?

A **feeling** is something happening inside a person's body and mind, like happy, sad, mad, or scared. Everyone has feelings, every single day — feelings are not good or bad, they just tell us something about what is happening around us.

Talking points for read-aloud:

- Ask the class: "What is one feeling you had today?"
- Explain that a feeling can change quickly — a child might feel excited, then a little worried, all in one morning.
- Remind students that having a feeling is never wrong. What matters is what we do with the feeling.

### Naming Feelings

**Naming feelings** means putting a word to what you feel inside, like saying "I feel happy" or "I feel frustrated." Naming a feeling out loud helps a child calm down and helps a trusted adult understand how to help.

The following table gives a few common feeling words a Kindergartner can practice naming, paired with a simple face clue a teacher can act out:

| Feeling Word | Face or Body Clue |
|---|---|
| Happy | Big smile, bouncy body |
| Sad | Downturned mouth, slow movements |
| Mad | Furrowed eyebrows, tight fists |
| Scared | Wide eyes, freezing still |
| Calm | Relaxed shoulders, slow breathing |

### Strong Feelings

A **strong feeling** is a feeling that is big and can feel uncomfortable, like being very scared, very angry, or very sad. Strong feelings can happen when something surprising, unfair, or scary happens. Everybody has strong feelings sometimes — even grown-ups!

A short list of situations that can cause strong feelings, useful for classroom discussion:

- Being teased or left out by other children
- A loud, sudden noise or a scary storm
- Getting hurt or seeing someone else get hurt
- A big change at home, like moving or a new sibling
- Losing a favorite toy or a pet

When a strong feeling shows up, a child's body might get a fast heartbeat, hot cheeks, or shaky hands. That is the body's normal way of reacting — it does not mean anything is wrong with the child.

### Asking For Help

**Asking for help** means telling a trusted adult when a feeling is too big to handle alone. This is one of the most important skills in this whole chapter: no child should ever have to sit with a strong, uncomfortable feeling by themselves.

Remind the class that a **trusted adult** — like the parents, teachers, and community helpers they met in Chapter 2 — is always ready to listen. Asking for help is never something to be embarrassed about; it is a brave, smart thing to do.

Talking points for read-aloud:

- Practice the exact words a child could say: "I feel scared, can you help me?" or "My tummy hurts because I'm worried."
- Explain that a trusted adult would much rather be asked ten small questions than have a child stay quiet about a big worry.
- Remind students that asking for help works for any feeling — happy feelings too! Sharing joy with a trusted adult makes it even better.

### Prolonged Sadness

Sometimes a strong feeling does not go away after a little while — it stays for many days. This is called **prolonged sadness**: feeling sad, worried, or upset for a long time instead of just for a little while.

!!! mascot-encouraging "If a Feeling Stays a Long Time"
    ![Scout encouraging](../../../../img/mascot/encouraging.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Most feelings come and go, like clouds passing by. But sometimes sadness or worry stays for many days in a row. If that happens to you, it is not your fault, and you are not alone. Telling a trusted adult when a feeling stays a long time is one of the most important things you can ever do. A trusted adult can help, and things can get better.

Guidance for teachers on this concept:

- This is sensitive content. Keep the tone calm, sincere, and reassuring — this is not the place for playful or dog-themed phrasing.
- Explain simply: "If you feel sad almost every day for many days, that is important to tell a trusted adult about, just like you would tell someone about a tummy ache that won't go away."
- Reassure the class that grown-ups have ways to help when sadness stays a long time, and that talking about it is always the right first step.
- This concept is an early, age-appropriate bridge toward mental health topics students will learn more about in later grades. The goal here is only: notice a feeling that stays a long time, and tell a trusted adult.

### Emotions Vocabulary

Building an **emotions vocabulary** means learning more and more words for feelings, beyond just happy, sad, mad, and scared. The more feeling words a child knows, the easier it is to tell a trusted adult exactly what is going on inside.

A short list of additional emotion words a teacher can introduce gradually, one or two at a time:

- Excited — a bouncy, eager happy feeling
- Frustrated — feeling stuck and annoyed when something is hard
- Proud — feeling good about something you did
- Lonely — feeling like you want company
- Nervous — a wobbly feeling before something new

### Kindness

**Kindness** means treating others in a warm, caring, and helpful way. Kindness is a choice a person makes, and it is one of the most powerful things a Kindergartner can practice every single day. Kindness is like planting a tiny seed — even a small kind action, like sharing a crayon, can grow into a really good day for someone else.

### Kind Behavior and Unkind Behavior

**Kind behavior** is an action that helps someone feel good, safe, or included, such as sharing, saying something nice, or helping a friend who fell down. **Unkind behavior** is an action that hurts someone's feelings or makes them feel bad, such as teasing, grabbing a toy, or saying mean words.

The following table compares a few examples of kind and unkind behavior side by side, useful for a classroom sorting discussion:

| Situation | Kind Behavior | Unkind Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| A friend falls down | Ask "Are you okay?" and help them up | Laugh or walk away |
| Someone wants to play | Say "Yes, come play with us!" | Say "No, go away" |
| A classmate is crying | Sit with them and offer comfort | Ignore them |
| Sharing crayons | Offer to take turns | Grab all the crayons |

### Including Others

**Including others** means making sure everyone nearby feels invited to join in, instead of being left out. Including others is one of the clearest ways a Kindergartner can show kindness during play.

A short list of ways to include others, useful for classroom modeling:

- Inviting a child who is playing alone to join the game
- Making room in a circle or at a table for one more friend
- Choosing teams so that everyone gets picked kindly, not left for last
- Asking a new student to sit together at lunch

### Wrap-Up for Teachers

By the end of this chapter, most Kindergartners should be able to name a feeling on their own face or a classmate's face, recall that asking a trusted adult for help is the right response to a strong or long-lasting feeling, and identify an example of kind versus unkind behavior. These are Remember- and early Understand-level goals, with the sorting and scenario MicroSims providing light Analyze- and Apply-level practice — no multi-step reasoning is expected yet.
