### What Is Conflict?

**Conflict** happens whenever two or more people want different things, disagree, or have a problem to work out together. Conflict is a normal part of life — it happens between friends, between brothers and sisters, and even between grown-ups. Having a conflict does not mean two people are bad friends; it just means they need to find a way to solve a problem.

Conflict can look like:

- Two friends both wanting to be first in line.
- Two students disagreeing about the rules of a game at recess.
- A brother and sister both wanting the same toy at the same time.
- A group project where classmates have different ideas about what to do.

### Healthy Conflict Resolution

**Healthy conflict resolution** means using calm, respectful steps to solve a disagreement so that everyone involved feels heard and the problem gets better instead of worse. Second graders can learn a simple, repeatable set of steps to use whenever a conflict comes up, whether it is at school, at home, or on the playground.

Here is a simple four-step process students can practice:

1. **Stop and take a breath.** Pause before reacting so you can think clearly.
2. **Say how you feel using an "I-statement."** For example, "I feel frustrated when we can't agree on the game."
3. **Listen to the other person's side.** Let them explain what they want and why.
4. **Find a solution together.** Try taking turns, compromising, or asking an adult to help if you get stuck.

Before we look at the diagram below, notice that these four steps always happen in the same order: calm down first, then talk, then listen, then solve. Skipping a step — like trying to "solve" before anyone has calmed down — usually makes the conflict harder to fix.

### Managing Strong Emotions

Sometimes a conflict — or something else entirely — brings up feelings that feel very big, like anger, frustration, or sadness. **Managing strong emotions** means noticing when a feeling is big and using a calming strategy before acting on it, instead of letting the feeling take over.

Big feelings are not bad. Everyone has them. What matters is what a person does next. Here are some strategies Grade 2 students can use to manage strong emotions:

- Take three slow, deep breaths.
- Count slowly to ten.
- Squeeze a stress ball or hug a stuffed animal.
- Walk away from the situation for a moment to cool down.
- Draw or write about the feeling.
- Ask for a hug or quiet moment with a trusted adult.

Managing a strong emotion is different from ignoring it. Ignoring a feeling means pretending it is not there; managing a feeling means noticing it, naming it, and choosing a calm way to work through it.

### Sharing Feelings With a Trusted Adult

Sometimes a feeling is too big to manage alone, or a conflict is too hard to solve without help. **Sharing feelings with a trusted adult** means telling a parent, teacher, or other safe grown-up what happened and how it made you feel, so they can help.

A trusted adult is someone a student and their family already know is safe and caring — often a parent, teacher, school counselor, or another caregiver. Sharing feelings with a trusted adult is not "tattling" — it is a healthy skill that helps solve problems that feel too big to handle alone.

A simple way to share a feeling with a trusted adult is to use an I-statement, the same skill from Chapter 1:

> "I feel ___ when ___ happens."

For example: "I feel sad when my friend teases me about my drawing."

Recall from Chapter 1 that active listening — giving full attention, making eye contact, and reflecting back what you heard — is also something a trusted adult uses when a student shares a feeling with them.

### Playful Interaction

Not every moment with friends involves conflict — most of the time, being with friends means **playful interaction**: joking, laughing, and having fun together in ways that feel good to everyone involved. Playful interaction is a big part of healthy friendships and healthy communities.

Kind, playful interaction usually shares a few features:

| Feature of Kind Play | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| Everyone is laughing together | No one feels left out or made fun of |
| Playing stops if someone says "stop" | Friends respect when a game or joke needs to end |
| Turns and rules are fair | Everyone gets a chance to play or lead |
| Words build people up | Jokes are about silly situations, not about a person's body, family, or abilities |

This table sums up a pattern worth remembering: kind, playful interaction always leaves everyone feeling included, not just some of the people involved. Playful interaction is meant to bring people closer together — if a "joke" pushes someone away or makes them feel small, it has stopped being playful.

### Teasing

**Teasing** is when someone makes a joking remark, often about another person, that can feel funny to one person but hurtful to another. Teasing sits right on the edge between playful interaction and something that can cause real harm, which is why it is important to understand it carefully.

There are different types of teasing, and they do not all feel the same:

- **Playful teasing between friends who both agree it is funny** — for example, gently joking about a shared inside joke that both people laugh about.
- **Teasing that targets something a person cannot easily change** — like their name, their family, how they look, or something they are working hard to learn.
- **Repeated teasing that continues after someone has asked it to stop.**

The second and third types of teasing are not healthy play, even if the person doing the teasing says "I was just joking." A joke that only one person finds funny is not truly playful — it is something else, and it deserves a caring, honest look rather than a light one.

!!! mascot-warning "An Important Difference"
    ![Scout warning](../../../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    If a joke keeps happening after someone has said "please stop" or shown they are hurt, it is not playful teasing anymore — it needs a trusted adult's help.

### Effects of Teasing

**Effects of teasing** refers to how teasing changes the way a person feels — about themselves, about the person teasing them, and about being part of the group. Understanding these effects helps students recognize when a joke has crossed the line from fun into harm, in themselves and in others.

Teasing that is unwanted can lead to feelings like:

1. Embarrassment or shame, even about something that is not actually a problem.
2. Sadness or loneliness, especially if the teasing happens in front of others.
3. Anger or frustration at being made fun of.
4. Worry about being teased again, which can make a person avoid places or people.

These effects do not stay only with one person. When teasing happens in a classroom or on a playground, it can change how safe the whole group feels — some students may start to worry they could be teased next, which can make a classroom or community feel less kind overall.

### Recognizing Feelings About Behaviors

The last skill in this chapter ties everything together. **Recognizing feelings about behaviors** means noticing and naming how a specific action — your own or someone else's — makes you or others feel, so you can decide what to do next.

This skill applies to everything covered in this chapter:

- Recognizing that a conflict is making you feel frustrated is the first step toward using healthy conflict resolution.
- Recognizing that a feeling is getting too big is the first step toward managing strong emotions.
- Recognizing that a joke made a friend feel small — even if it was meant to be funny — is the first step toward understanding the effects of teasing.

A simple habit for practicing this skill is to pause after any interaction and ask: "How did that make me feel? How might it have made the other person feel?" Comparing your own reaction to how someone else might have reacted is exactly the kind of comparison skill Grade 2 students are building this year. Try asking that question after playtime, a disagreement, or a joke — noticing your own feelings is a skill that gets stronger every time you practice it.
