### What Is Unwanted Touch?

**Unwanted touch** is any touch that a person does not want, that makes a person feel uncomfortable, confused, or unsafe, or that involves a private part of the body in a way that is not okay. A touch does not have to hurt to be unwanted — it can feel unwanted even if it is gentle, even if it comes from someone the child knows and likes, and even if the other person says it is a game or a secret.

It helps students to know that touches generally fall into different categories, and that the same touch can be welcome from one situation and unwanted in another:

- A safe, wanted touch feels good and the person giving it is someone the child trusts and wants to be touched by, like a hug from a parent or a high-five from a friend.
- An unwanted touch is any touch a person does not want — no matter who gives it, no matter how it is described, and no matter whether it hurts or not.
- A confusing touch might not feel painful or even feel "bad" in the moment, but it still counts as unwanted if it involves private body parts, feels secretive, or makes a stomach feel funny.

The most important idea in this section is this: **the child's own feeling is what matters.** A student does not need to prove that a touch was wrong, explain exactly why it felt bad, or wait until they are certain. If a touch feels unwanted, it is okay — and important — to say so.

    Here is the most important idea in this whole chapter: your body belongs to you. You get to decide who touches you and how. If a touch ever feels wrong, confusing, or unwanted — even a little — you are allowed to say so.

Unwanted touch can come from someone a child does not know well, but it can also come from someone familiar — a relative, a family friend, an older student, or another trusted person. This is an uncomfortable truth, but it is an important one: **familiarity does not make a touch safe.** The deciding factor is never who the person is — it is always whether the touch is wanted.

One more idea deserves special attention, because it is one of the most common tactics used to keep a child silent: being asked to keep a touch a secret from a trusted adult.

### Saying No Or Exiting

**Saying no or exiting** means using words, a firm voice, or simply walking away to stop an unwanted touch or leave a situation that feels unsafe. A student does not need permission to do either of these things — they can say no, they can leave, and they can do both at the same time, even without explaining why in the moment.

There are a few simple, practiced ways to say no or exit that work in almost any situation:

1. Use a strong, clear voice: "No," "Stop," or "I don't like that."
2. Step or move away from the person and the situation right away.
3. Go find a trusted adult as quickly as possible.
4. Keep telling adults until one listens and helps — the first adult told does not always have to be the last.

That fourth step matters just as much as the first three. Occasionally a child's first attempt to tell may not be fully understood right away — perhaps the adult is busy, or does not immediately grasp what is being described. The lesson for a Grade 2 student is not to give up, but to keep telling a trusted adult, or a different trusted adult, until someone listens and helps.

A student practicing "saying no or exiting" should know that it can look different depending on the moment — sometimes it is a loud, firm "No!," sometimes it is quietly getting up and leaving the room, and sometimes it is both at once. There is no single "right" way to do it, as long as it gets the child away from the unwanted touch and toward a trusted adult.

### Wrap-Up for Teachers

By the end of this chapter, most Grade 2 students should be able to describe what makes a touch feel unwanted and demonstrate at least one way to say no or exit such a situation, followed by telling a trusted adult. Because this is core child-abuse-prevention content, plan to revisit this lesson more than once during the year rather than teaching it only a single time. Reinforce the three anchor messages in every retelling: a child's body belongs to them, they always have the right to say no or leave, and they should always tell a trusted adult — even if told to keep a secret, and even if they worry about getting in trouble. Consider pairing this chapter with your school counselor or school-based prevention curriculum, and make sure students know, by name, at least one trusted adult at school they can go to if a parent or guardian is not available.
