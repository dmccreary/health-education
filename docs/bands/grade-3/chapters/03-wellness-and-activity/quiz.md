#### 1. What is "traditional health knowledge"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A rule that only doctors can use
2. A myth that isn't actually true
3. A brand-new idea invented last year
4. Wisdom that cultures and communities have developed over generations for protecting the body
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Traditional health knowledge is wisdom that different cultures and communities have developed over generations for protecting the body from the environment, such as special clothing, foods, or daily habits. It is real, valuable knowledge.

    **Concept Tested:** Traditional Health Knowledge

#### 2. Layered animal-skin and fur clothing, used traditionally by Arctic Indigenous communities, mainly protects the body from what?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Strong sun
2. Extreme cold
3. Upset stomachs
4. Loud noises
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Layered animal-skin and fur clothing traps warm air against the body far better than a single thick coat, protecting Arctic Indigenous communities against extreme cold.

    **Concept Tested:** Traditional Health Knowledge

#### 5. What is the "layering idea" for dressing in cold weather?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Wearing one very thick coat instead of anything else
2. Wearing several thinner layers that trap warm air and can be adjusted
3. Wearing shorts no matter the temperature
4. Wearing swimwear under regular clothes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The layering idea means wearing two or three thinner layers instead of one thick coat. The layers trap warm air between them and can be adjusted by adding or removing a layer throughout the day.

    **Concept Tested:** Dressing For Weather

#### 7. What are "cultural germ prevention practices"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

3. Games played only in gym class
4. Customs and habits communities use to reduce the spread of illness-causing germs

#### 8. Removing shoes before entering a home mainly blocks which path that germs use to travel?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The path from one person's hands directly to another's
2. The path from outdoor dirt on shoes to indoor floors
3. The path through the air from a cough
4. The path from food to a plate
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Removing shoes blocks the path from outdoor dirt on shoes to indoor floors and hands, keeping outdoor germs from spreading onto floors where people sit, eat, and play.

    **Concept Tested:** Why Germ Prevention Practices Work

#### 9. Why do many cultures teach covering a cough or sneeze with an elbow instead of a bare hand?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It looks more polite in photographs
2. It keeps clothing cleaner
3. It blocks droplets from spreading into the air or onto hands
4. It makes the cough quieter
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Covering a cough or sneeze with an elbow or cloth blocks droplets from spreading into the air or onto hands, protecting both the sneezer and the people around them.

    **Concept Tested:** Cultural Germ Prevention Practices

#### 10. Why do germ prevention practices, like hand washing or removing shoes, actually work?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Germs cannot travel on their own, so blocking their path (hands, air, surfaces) stops them from spreading
2. They make rooms smell better
3. They are simply old traditions with no real reason
4. They only work in cold weather
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Germs cannot travel on their own — they need to be carried by hands, droplets in the air, or surfaces people touch. Each cultural practice blocks a different path germs use to spread between people.

    **Concept Tested:** Why Germ Prevention Practices Work
