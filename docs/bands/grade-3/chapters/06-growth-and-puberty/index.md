## Puberty

Every person who has ever lived has gone through the same basic stage of growth: a time when the body changes from a child's body into a more grown-up body. This stage has a name.

**Puberty** is the normal, healthy stage of growth when a child's body gradually begins changing into a more adult body, including growing taller, developing new body shapes, and experiencing new feelings. Puberty happens to every human body eventually — it is not something that happens to only some people or that can be avoided. It is simply part of being human, the same way that learning to walk or losing baby teeth was part of growing up earlier in life.

One of the most important facts about puberty is timing: it does not start on the same day, month, or even year for everyone. Some classmates will begin noticing changes while they are still in elementary school; others will not notice changes until they are in middle school. Both are completely normal. There is no "correct" age for puberty to begin, and starting earlier or later than a friend does not mean anything is wrong.

!!! mascot-thinking "Big Idea"
    ![Scout thinking](../../../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Puberty happens at a different pace for every person — even between identical twins. That means there is no "right" time for it to start. Every timeline is a normal timeline.

During puberty, a young person's body and feelings change in a few general ways:

- **Growth spurts** — growing taller and heavier faster than in earlier years
- **Body shape changes** — the body gradually beginning to take on a more adult shape
- **Skin changes** — skin may become oilier, and some young people notice more sweat or body odor
- **Emotional changes** — feelings can feel stronger, more mixed-up, or more sudden than before

Puberty is guided by natural chemical messengers inside the body called hormones, but a young person doesn't need to memorize the science to understand the big picture: this is a normal, healthy stage of growth, and every body goes through it in its own way and its own time.

It is also completely normal to feel a mix of emotions about puberty — some students feel excited about growing up, some feel nervous, some feel confused, and many feel several of these feelings on the very same day. None of those feelings are wrong.

## Stages Of Puberty

Puberty is not a single moment — it is a gradual process that unfolds over several years. Thinking about it in simple stages can help students understand what "going through puberty" actually means.

**Stages of puberty** are the general, simplified phases young people move through during this stage of growth: a time before changes start, a time when changes are just beginning, and a time when changes continue and become more noticeable. These stages are not exact ages and do not look identical for every person — they are simply a helpful way to describe the general order that growth tends to follow.

A simple way to think about the three general stages:

1. **Before changes start** — the body is still growing the way it has throughout childhood, without puberty changes yet.
2. **Changes beginning** — a young person may start noticing small changes, such as growing a bit faster or new feelings that seem stronger than before.
3. **Changes continuing** — changes become more noticeable over time, such as continued growth spurts and body shape changes, as the body continues moving toward its adult form.

It's worth repeating an important point from the first section: no two people move through these three general stages at exactly the same age. A student might be in the "changes beginning" stage while a same-age classmate is still in "before changes start," and both are exactly where they should be.

Knowing that puberty happens in a general order is useful, but the most important skill in this whole chapter isn't a fact to memorize — it's knowing exactly what to do when a body change feels confusing, and who to turn to.

## Asking For Help With Body Changes

With so many new changes happening, it is completely normal for a young person to have questions — and just as normal to feel a little unsure about how to ask them.

**Asking for help with body changes** means telling a trusted adult about a physical or emotional change, or a question about growing up, so that adult can explain what is happening and offer support. This is a skill, just like asking for help with a hard math problem or a scraped knee — the more a student practices it, the easier it becomes.

There is no such thing as a silly question about growing up. A trusted adult would always rather answer a question — even one asked more than once — than have a student worry alone. Some examples of the kinds of things a student might ask a trusted adult:

- "Is it normal that I feel like crying for no reason some days?"
- "My friend already started changing and I haven't — is something wrong with me?"
- "I noticed my body doing something new. Is that normal?"
- "I feel nervous about growing up. Is that okay?"

The answer to every single one of these questions is: yes, that's normal, and a trusted adult can talk it through.

## Identifying Trusted Adults

Asking for help only works if a student first knows *who* counts as a trusted adult for this kind of question. This chapter builds directly on the trusted-adult idea from Chapter 2, and applies it specifically to body changes and growing up.

**Identifying trusted adults** means recognizing which grown-ups in a student's life are safe, caring, and appropriate to go to with questions or concerns about body changes and growing up — such as a parent or guardian, another close family member, a school nurse, a teacher, or a doctor. A trusted adult for this topic is someone who listens without making a student feel embarrassed, answers honestly, and keeps the conversation calm and supportive.

A few signs that someone is a good trusted adult to ask about body changes:

| Sign | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| Listens fully | Gives full attention instead of rushing the conversation |
| Answers honestly | Gives calm, truthful answers instead of avoiding the topic |
| Keeps students comfortable | Never teases or makes a student feel embarrassed for asking |
| Is available | Can be reached at home, at school, or at a doctor's visit |

Every student's list of trusted adults may look a little different, and that is completely fine — the important thing is that every student has at least one adult they feel comfortable asking. If the very first trusted adult a student asks seems busy or the moment doesn't feel right, that's okay — trying a different trusted adult, or trying again later, is a perfectly good next step.
