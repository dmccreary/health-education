## Verbal Communication

Every day, students share thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs with the people around them. The words a person chooses, and how those words are said, make a big difference in whether the message is understood.

**Verbal communication** means using spoken or written words to share thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs with another person. It includes not just *what* is said, but *how* it is said — the tone of voice, the volume, and the speed of speaking all shape how a message lands.

A student using strong verbal communication:

- Uses clear, specific words instead of vague ones ("I don't understand the third math problem" instead of just "This is hard")
- Speaks in a calm, steady voice, even when a feeling is strong
- Waits for a good moment to speak, rather than interrupting
- Says thoughts, feelings, wants, and needs directly, instead of hinting

!!! mascot-thinking "Big Idea"
    ![Scout thinking](../../../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Clear words help other people understand exactly what's going on inside a person's head — and that's the first step to getting help, working things out, or sharing good news.

Words are only part of the story, though. The very next section explores the messages people send without saying a single word.

## Nonverbal Communication

Long before a person finishes a sentence, their face, posture, and hands are already sending a message.

**Nonverbal communication** means sharing thoughts and feelings through facial expressions, body language, gestures, eye contact, and posture, without using words at all. Nonverbal messages can support spoken words, or they can accidentally contradict them — like saying "I'm fine" while frowning and crossing arms.

## Active Listening

Good communication is a two-way street. A student can speak clearly and read body language well, and still miss the point if they aren't truly listening.

**Active listening** means fully paying attention to a speaker — with eyes, body, and mind — instead of just waiting for a turn to talk. Active listening includes making eye contact, nodding, staying quiet while the other person speaks, and checking understanding by repeating back what was heard.

Here is a comparison of active listening and its opposite:

| Active Listening Looks Like | Not Listening Looks Like |
|---|---|
| Facing the speaker and making eye contact | Looking at a screen or elsewhere |
| Waiting for a pause before responding | Interrupting mid-sentence |
| Nodding or saying "I hear you" | Staring blankly or looking bored |
| Asking a follow-up question | Changing the subject right away |
| Repeating back the main idea to check | Assuming you already know what they'll say |

## Communication In Decision-Making

Many decisions involve more than one person — choosing a group project topic, deciding what game to play at recess, or figuring out a plan with a family member. Communication skills make those moments go much more smoothly.

**Communication in decision-making** means using verbal communication, nonverbal communication, and active listening together to share opinions, hear other people's opinions, and reach a decision that works for everyone involved. This might mean explaining a preference clearly, listening to someone else's reason, and being willing to compromise.

A simple process for communicating well during a group decision:

1. Say what you think or want, clearly and calmly
2. Actively listen to what others think or want
3. Notice everyone's nonverbal cues (excited? unsure? frustrated?)
4. Look for an option that respects as many people's needs as possible
5. Agree on a decision together, or ask a trusted adult to help if the group is stuck

    ![Scout thinking](../../../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Good decision-making with others isn't about who talks the loudest — it's about everyone getting a real turn to speak and be heard.

## I-Statement

Sometimes a person needs to share a feeling or a need in a moment that already feels a little tense — like when a friend borrowed something without asking, or a sibling turned off a TV show mid-episode. The words chosen in that moment matter enormously.

**An I-statement** is a way of expressing a thought, feeling, want, or need that starts with "I" and focuses on the speaker's own experience, rather than blaming or accusing the other person. A typical pattern is: *"I feel [feeling] when [situation happens], because [reason]. Could you [request]?"* I-statements tend to keep conversations calm, because they describe an experience instead of attacking a person.

Here is a comparison of blaming statements and I-statements:

| Blaming Statement (Y0u-Statement) | I-Statement |
|---|---|
| "You always take my stuff without asking!" | "I feel frustrated when my things are borrowed without asking. Could you ask me first next time?" |
| "You never listen to me!" | "I feel unheard when I'm interrupted. Could I finish my thought?" |
| "You ruined the game!" | "I feel disappointed that the game ended early. Can we plan a rule for next time?" |

## Personal Health Goal

Everything communicated clearly — a feeling, a need, a decision — can also be aimed at something bigger: a goal a student sets for their own health.

**A personal health goal** is a specific, health-related behavior a student chooses to work on, such as drinking more water, going to bed earlier, or trying a new fruit or vegetable each week. A strong personal health goal is specific (naming exactly what will change), realistic (something the student can actually do), and personal (chosen because it matters to that student, not just assigned by someone else).

Examples of well-formed personal health goals:

- "I will drink a full glass of water with breakfast, lunch, and dinner this week."
- "I will go to bed 15 minutes earlier on school nights."
- "I will practice one deep-breathing coping strategy when I feel frustrated."
- "I will try one new vegetable at dinner this week."

Choosing a goal is an exciting first step — but not every goal can be reached alone, which is exactly what the next section helps a student figure out.

## Determining Need For Assistance

Some goals are easy to work on independently, like remembering to drink more water. Others go much better with someone else's help, like changing a bedtime routine that depends on the whole family's schedule.

**Determining need for assistance** means thinking honestly about a personal health goal and deciding whether it can be reached alone, or whether it would go better with help or collaboration from a parent, teacher, coach, or other trusted adult. Recognizing when help is useful is not a weakness — it is a smart, realistic part of goal-setting.

Questions a student can ask to figure this out:

- Does reaching this goal depend on someone else's schedule or decisions (like bedtime or meals)?
- Would it help to have someone check in and remind me?
- Is there a part of this goal I don't know how to do yet?
- Would celebrating progress with someone else keep me motivated?

If the answer to any of these is "yes," asking for assistance is a great next step.

??? note "Quick Check for Read-Aloud Time — Click to expand"
    Ask the class: "If a student's goal is to go to bed 15 minutes earlier, who might they need help from, and why?" Then reveal: "A parent or guardian, because bedtime often depends on the whole family's evening routine — asking for their help makes the goal much more realistic."

## Examining Health-Supporting Practices

A single goal is a great start, but it helps to first take a wider look at all the everyday practices that support health, before narrowing in on just one.

**Examining health-supporting practices** means looking closely at the everyday behaviors that support a person's physical, mental, and emotional health, in order to notice which practices are already going well and which ones might need attention. This connects nearly every topic from this entire book — food choices, physical activity, sleep, safety habits, communication, and coping skills all count as health-supporting practices.

## Identifying Relevant Health Topics

Not every health topic feels equally important to every student at every moment — and that's expected. Part of taking charge of one's own health is noticing which topics feel most relevant right now.

**Identifying relevant health topics** means recognizing which health topics feel most meaningful or useful to a student and the people around them at this point in their life, so that attention and effort can be focused where they matter most. A topic that feels very relevant to one student, like managing nervousness before a big event, might feel less urgent to another student focused on building a new sleep routine.
