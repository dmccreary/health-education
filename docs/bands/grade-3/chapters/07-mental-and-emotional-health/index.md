## Resilience

Every person has hard days — a friend might move away, a pet might get sick, or a test might not go the way a student hoped. What matters most is not whether hard things happen, since they happen to everyone, but what a person does next.

**Resilience** is the ability to recover from a hard day, a disappointment, or a difficult situation, and keep going in a healthy way. Resilience does not mean never feeling sad, mad, or discouraged — it means feeling those feelings and then finding a path forward instead of staying stuck. Resilience is a skill that grows stronger with practice.

A student building resilience might:

- Notice and name the hard feeling ("I feel disappointed that I didn't make the team.")
- Remind themselves that one hard moment does not erase all the good things in their life
- Try again, ask for help, or try a different approach
- Give themselves credit for trying, even when the outcome wasn't perfect

!!! mascot-thinking "Big Idea"

    Resilience isn't about ignoring hard feelings — it's about moving through them. Bending like a tree in the wind is strong. Trying never to feel the wind at all isn't possible for anyone.

## Personal Choices And Wellbeing

Some parts of mental and emotional wellbeing are outside a student's control, like whether a big change happens in their family. But many parts are shaped by small, everyday choices.

**Personal choices and wellbeing** describes the connection between the decisions a person makes each day — such as how much sleep they get, how they move their body, who they spend time with, and how they talk to themselves — and how healthy their mind and emotions feel over time. "Wellbeing" simply means an overall sense of feeling okay, physically, mentally, and emotionally, most days, and choices that support it don't have to be complicated.

Here is a look at how everyday choices tend to affect wellbeing:

| Choice | Effect on Wellbeing |
|---|---|
| Getting enough sleep | Improves mood, focus, and patience with others |
| Being physically active | Releases feelings of stress and boosts mood |
| Spending time with caring people | Builds a sense of belonging and support |
| Talking about feelings instead of bottling them up | Prevents feelings from building into bigger problems |
| Taking quiet breaks from screens | Gives the brain time to rest and reset |

A student cannot control every event in their life, but small choices, repeated daily, add up to a big difference in how someone feels.

## Coping With Challenges

A challenging situation is any moment that brings up strong or uncomfortable feelings — frustration over a hard homework problem, sadness about an argument with a friend, or worry about a change at home. Facing challenges is normal, and there are healthy ways to move through them.

**Coping with challenges** means using healthy strategies to manage the strong feelings that come with a difficult situation, instead of letting those feelings take over or ignoring them completely.

Some healthy coping strategies a student can try:

1. Take slow, deep breaths and count to ten
2. Name the feeling out loud or write it down
3. Go for a walk, stretch, or move the body
4. Draw, write, or make something
5. Talk to someone who cares

!!! mascot-tip "Helpful Tip"
    ![Scout giving a tip](../../../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Coping strategies work best when practiced *before* a hard moment happens — like a fire drill. Trying a few strategies out on an ordinary day makes them easier to remember on a hard one.

Coping strategies help in the moment, but knowing who to bring bigger feelings to is just as important a skill.

## Who To Talk To

Some feelings are light enough to handle with a quick coping strategy. Others are heavier and work best when shared with someone else — and that is not a sign of weakness. It is one of the healthiest things a person can do.

**Who to talk to** refers to identifying the trusted people in a student's life — such as a parent or guardian, another family member, a teacher, a school counselor, or a school nurse — who can be told about a hard feeling so they can help. This builds directly on the trusted-adult idea from earlier in this book: the same people who help with safety questions can also help with feelings. A good person to talk to about feelings listens without judging, takes the feeling seriously, and helps figure out a next step.

Practicing what to say can make starting the conversation much easier. A few sentence starters a student can use:

- "Can I talk to you about something that's bothering me?"
- "I've been feeling really [sad/worried/frustrated] lately."
- "Something happened and I don't know how to feel about it."

Any trusted adult who hears one of these openers will know exactly how to help. Sometimes, though, a hard feeling isn't just a single tough day — it can last longer and need a different kind of understanding, which is what the next two sections explain.

## Mental Health Challenge

Just like some people have ongoing physical health needs — such as needing glasses to see clearly, or medicine to manage allergies — some people have ongoing needs related to their thoughts, feelings, or behavior.

**A mental health challenge** is an ongoing difficulty with thoughts, feelings, or behavior that affects how a person feels or acts in daily life, and that can be helped by talking with trusted adults or health professionals. Examples students may have heard of include feeling worried much of the time (anxiety) or feeling very sad for a long stretch of time (depression). A mental health challenge is not something a person chooses, and a person cannot simply decide to stop having one, just like a person cannot decide to stop needing glasses.

A few important, plain facts about mental health challenges:

- They are common. Many people — children and adults — experience a mental health challenge at some point in their lives.
- They are not anyone's fault. A person does not cause their own mental health challenge, and nothing is "wrong" with someone who has one.
- They can be helped. Talking with a trusted adult, a school counselor, or a doctor is the first step, and there are many ways professionals can help.
- They are private and personal. A person gets to decide how much to share about their own experience, and everyone deserves privacy and respect around this topic.

Having a mental health challenge is a normal part of some people's lives, the same way needing help with reading or needing a cast for a broken arm is normal. It is nothing to feel ashamed of, and trusted adults and health professionals are there to help.

## Effects Of Mental Health Challenges

**Effects of mental health challenges** are the ways an ongoing difficulty with thoughts, feelings, or behavior can show up in a person's daily life, such as trouble concentrating in class, changes in sleep or appetite, pulling away from friends, or feeling tired even after resting. These effects look different from person to person, and can also affect the people who care about that person. A classmate showing one of these signs is not choosing to act that way, and deserves the same patience a student would want if they were struggling with something hard.

| What It Might Look Like | What It Does Not Mean |
|---|---|
| Trouble focusing on schoolwork | The person isn't trying hard enough |
| Pulling away from friends | The person doesn't like their friends anymore |
| Seeming tired, sad, or worried often | The person is being dramatic |
| Changes in eating or sleeping | The person is doing it on purpose |

The most helpful response to a classmate, friend, or family member showing these signs is the same skill from earlier in this chapter: encouraging them to talk to a trusted adult, and being a caring, non-judgmental friend while they get support. Trusted adults and health professionals, such as school counselors, doctors, and therapists, are specially trained to help people manage mental health challenges, and asking for that help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

## Empathy

Understanding what someone else might be feeling, especially a feeling a student has never personally experienced, is one of the most valuable skills a person can build.

**Empathy** is the ability to notice, understand, and care about what another person is feeling, even when that feeling is different from what the student is feeling in that moment. Empathy is different from simply feeling sorry for someone; it means genuinely trying to imagine the situation from the other person's point of view.

Empathy can be practiced in small, everyday moments:

- Noticing when a classmate looks upset and checking in on them
- Imagining how a new student might feel on their first day
- Pausing before reacting to ask, "What might they be feeling right now?"

    Empathy can feel hard at first, especially for feelings a student hasn't experienced themselves. That's completely okay — empathy is a muscle that gets stronger every time it's used.

Once a student understands empathy in general, it becomes easier to see exactly how it shows up in one of the most important parts of their daily life: friendship.

## Empathy In Friendships

Friendships are built and strengthened through many small moments of noticing and caring about a friend's feelings.

**Empathy in friendships** means using empathy specifically to support and care for friends — noticing when a friend is struggling, celebrating with a friend who is excited, and responding to a friend's feelings with kindness rather than dismissing them. Empathy is one of the biggest reasons some friendships feel warm and supportive while others feel one-sided.

## Emotions Influence Behavior

Feelings and actions are closely linked. Recognizing that link is one of the most useful things a student can understand about themselves.

**Emotions influence behavior** describes the way a person's feelings shape what they do next — for example, frustration might lead to snapping at someone, while excitement might lead to talking very fast or wanting to share good news right away. Noticing this connection gives a student the power to pause and choose a helpful action instead of letting a strong feeling take over automatically.

A simple way to picture the link: Feeling → Noticing the feeling → Choosing a response → Action. For example: feeling frustrated → noticing "I'm getting frustrated" → deciding to take a breath instead of yelling → calmly asking for help. That pause between noticing a feeling and choosing an action is exactly where resilience and coping strategies from earlier in this chapter come into play.

## Expressing Emotions

Once a student notices a feeling, the next skill is deciding how to share it with others in a way that helps rather than hurts.

**Expressing emotions** means communicating feelings to others in healthy ways — through words, tone of voice, facial expressions, or body language — instead of bottling feelings up or letting them come out as hurtful words or actions. Healthy expression names the feeling calmly and clearly; unhealthy expression either hides the feeling completely or lets it explode onto someone else.

| Feeling | Unhealthy Expression | Healthy Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Frustration | Yelling or slamming things | "I feel frustrated because this is hard for me." |
| Sadness | Hiding it and pretending to be fine | "I feel sad about what happened today." |
| Excitement | Interrupting everyone to talk only about it | "I'm so excited — can I tell you something?" |
| Jealousy | Saying mean things about the other person | "I feel jealous, and I want to talk about why." |

## Kindness And Respect For All Abilities

Every classroom is full of people whose minds and bodies work in different ways. Some students learn to read at a different pace than others, some use a wheelchair, hearing aid, or other tool, and some think, focus, or process the world differently — sometimes called being neurodivergent, which includes differences like ADHD, autism, or dyslexia. None of these differences make a person less valuable, less capable, or less deserving of friendship.

**Kindness and respect for all abilities** means treating every person with care and fairness regardless of how their body or mind works, recognizing that different does not mean less — it simply means different. This includes both physical disabilities and differences in how a person's brain processes information, learns, or focuses.

A few ways students can show this kind of respect every day:

- Ask a classmate how they'd like to be helped, instead of assuming
- Include classmates with disabilities or differences in games and group work
- Never tease, exclude, or mock the way someone talks, moves, learns, or focuses
- Remember that a tool someone uses — like a wheelchair, hearing aid, or fidget tool — is just a tool, the same way glasses are a tool
- Speak directly to the classmate, not just to their helper or aide
