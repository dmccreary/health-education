# Quiz: Managing Emotions And Relationships

Test your understanding of self-management strategies, triggers, relationships, and supporting a peer with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does the word "restorative" mean in the phrase "restorative self-management strategies"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The strategy proves the feeling was wrong to have
2. The strategy replaces one bad feeling with a worse one
3. The strategy makes a feeling disappear forever
4. The strategy helps you recover from a strong feeling so you can think clearly again
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Restorative strategies don't erase a feeling or pretend it didn't happen. They help a person recover from a strong emotion so they can think clearly and make good choices next, rather than acting on the feeling right away.

    **Concept Tested:** Restorative Self-Management Strategies

---

#### 2. Which of these is an example of a body-calming strategy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Taking slow breaths or counting to ten
2. Writing in a journal about what happened
3. Talking to a trusted adult about the situation
4. Naming the emotion out loud
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Body-calming strategies work directly on the body, like slow breathing, counting to ten, or taking a short walk. The other options are examples of expressing or reflecting strategies, which are also useful but work in a different way.

    **Concept Tested:** Restorative Self-Management Strategies

---

#### 3. Why don't restorative strategies work like an instant switch that turns a strong feeling off?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because feelings are not real
2. Because they require practicing before the moment you really need them
3. Because only trusted adults can use them
4. Because they only work for people over age 18
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Restorative strategies work because you practice them before you desperately need them, so they feel familiar in the moment a strong emotion actually hits. Waiting until a hard moment to try a new strategy for the first time makes it much harder to use.

    **Concept Tested:** Restorative Self-Management Strategies

---

#### 4. In the Emotion Weather Map idea, why might the same small trigger (a friend canceling plans) cause a bigger reaction during a "stormy" week than a "sunny" week?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Weather actually changes how emotions work
2. Triggers always produce the exact same reaction no matter what else is happening
3. Ongoing situations like poor sleep or family stress can make a person more sensitive to triggers
4. A stormy week means the trigger did not really happen
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Broader ongoing situations, like a stressful week at home or poor sleep, shape a person's emotional baseline. This means the very same small trigger can land much harder during a hard week than during a good one.

    **Concept Tested:** Situations That Influence Emotions

---

#### 5. Why is respecting a friend's boundary about privacy different from staying silent about a safety concern?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Boundaries and safety concerns are exactly the same thing
2. You should always keep every secret a friend tells you
3. Safety concerns should never be shared with anyone
4. Ordinary struggles can stay private, but information about someone's safety must go to a trusted adult
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Respecting boundaries protects a friend's privacy about ordinary struggles, but it never means staying silent about something that puts their safety at risk. If a friend might be in danger, that information must go to a trusted adult even if the friend asked for secrecy.

    **Concept Tested:** Respecting Peer Boundaries

#### 6. Why does the chapter say that conflict itself isn't the problem?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because unmanaged conflict, not disagreement itself, is what damages a relationship
2. Because conflict never happens between real friends
3. Because every conflict ends a friendship
4. Because conflict should always be avoided completely
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Every relationship experiences some friction. Two friends can disagree and still walk away with a stronger friendship if they handle the disagreement with respect — it's unmanaged conflict, not conflict itself, that causes lasting harm.

    **Concept Tested:** Managing Relationships

#### 7. Your friend shares that she's been struggling, and it's not a safety concern, but she asks you not to tell anyone. What is the best response, based on respecting peer boundaries?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Tell a teacher immediately regardless of what she asked
2. Honor her request for now, while letting her know you're there and encouraging her to talk to someone when ready
3. Pressure her to explain every detail right away
4. Tell other classmates so they can help support her
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Respecting peer boundaries means honoring a friend's pace and privacy about ordinary struggles, while still letting them know you care and gently encouraging them toward more support when they're ready.

    **Concept Tested:** Respecting Peer Boundaries

#### 8. Which sentence best demonstrates using an "I feel" statement during a disagreement?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "You always ruin everything."
2. "Everyone thinks you're being unfair."
3. "I felt left out when the plan changed without telling me."
4. "This is all your fault."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. An "I feel" statement describes your own experience without blaming the other person, which helps a disagreement get resolved instead of escalating. The other options all place blame directly on the other person.

    **Concept Tested:** Managing Relationships

#### 9. A classmate mentions they're sad but doesn't want to talk about it in detail. Which response best supports them without trying to fix their whole problem?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "Just try to be happier, it's not a big deal."
2. "Tell me every detail so I can solve it for you."
3. "I don't want to hear about it right now."
4. "That sounds really hard. I'm glad you told me, and I'm here if you want to talk."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Supporting a peer in difficulty means offering kindness and a listening ear without trying to solve their whole problem. This response is caring and present without pressuring them for details they aren't ready to share.

    **Concept Tested:** Supporting A Peer In Difficulty

#### 10. A student snaps angrily at a classmate over a small comment, right after a week of poor sleep and family stress at home. What does this best illustrate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A broader ongoing situation can add weight to how strongly a small trigger lands
2. The classmate's comment was the only cause of the reaction
3. Sleep has no connection to emotional reactions
4. Anger is always an overreaction and should be hidden
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A big reaction to a small trigger often isn't really about that small thing at all — the surrounding situation, like poor sleep and family stress, adds weight to it. Recognizing this helps students respond to their own and others' big feelings with more patience.

    **Concept Tested:** Situations That Influence Emotions
