## What Is Health?

You have studied health before, but this year the idea gets bigger. **Health** is the overall condition of your body and mind, including your physical, mental, and social well-being — not just whether you are free from illness. A student who eats well but never talks to anyone about a worry is not fully healthy. A student who feels emotionally supported but never sleeps or moves their body is not fully healthy either. Health is a combination of pieces working together.

Think of health as having three connected parts:

- **Physical health** — how your body is functioning: energy, sleep, nutrition, physical activity, and freedom from illness or injury.
- **Mental and emotional health** — how you think, feel, and handle stress, including your ability to name and manage emotions.
- **Social health** — the quality of your relationships with family, friends, and your community, and how well you communicate and get support.

These three parts constantly affect each other. A stressful argument with a friend (social) can make it hard to sleep (physical), which can make you feel irritable the next day (emotional). This chapter focuses on the connections between your relationships, your emotions, and your decisions — because understanding those connections is the foundation for nearly everything else in this book.

## Family Influence on Decisions

No one makes health decisions completely alone. **Family influence on decisions** describes how the beliefs, habits, rules, and reactions of the people you live with shape the choices you make about food, sleep, screen time, friendships, and more. Family influence can be direct, like a parent setting a bedtime, or indirect, like noticing that everyone in your household eats dinner together and quietly deciding that feels normal and good.

Family influence works in more than one direction, and it is not automatically good or bad — it depends on the specific habit or message.

- A family that models regular physical activity, like walking after dinner, makes that choice feel normal and easy to copy.
- A family that talks openly about feelings can make it easier for a student to name and manage their own emotions.
- A family stress, like a parent working long hours or a household disagreement, can shape a student's mood and behavior even when no one intends it to.
- Family traditions and culture shape ideas about food, celebration, rest, and even what counts as "being healthy," and these traditions vary widely and validly across households.

Recognizing family influence does not mean blaming your family — it means noticing the pattern so you can decide, thoughtfully, which habits to keep and which ones you might want to build differently for yourself.

## Peer Influence on Decisions

As you get older, a second powerful influence grows alongside family: your peers. **Peer influence on decisions** describes how friends and classmates affect the choices you make, through direct suggestions, group habits, or simply wanting to fit in. Peer influence intensifies during upper elementary and middle school years as friendships take up more emotional space in a student's life.

Like family influence, peer influence is not automatically positive or negative.

| Peer Influence Example | Effect on Decisions |
|---|---|
| A friend invites you to join a sports team | Can build a positive habit of regular physical activity |
| A classmate pressures you to skip homework to hang out | Can pull a decision away from a responsibility |
| A friend group normalizes talking through disagreements calmly | Can build a positive conflict-resolution habit |
| A peer teases someone for their food choices at lunch | Can create pressure to eat differently just to avoid teasing |

Notice that peer influence often works through everyday small moments, not big dramatic events. Before we compare family and peer influence directly, it helps to hold both definitions clearly in mind: family influence comes from the people who raise and live with you, while peer influence comes from friends and classmates who are usually the same age.

## Emotions Shape Behavior

Family and peers are outside influences. There is also a powerful influence that happens entirely inside you: your emotions. **Emotions shape behavior** describes how a feeling — happiness, anger, embarrassment, excitement, worry, jealousy, or any other emotion — often leads directly to an action, sometimes before you even notice you are reacting. A student who feels embarrassed after making a mistake in class might avoid raising their hand for weeks afterward. A student who feels excited about a friend's invitation might blurt out a "yes" before checking with a parent.

Certain situations, people, and experiences reliably trigger strong emotions for most students: being left out, being praised in front of others, an argument with a sibling, a big test, a change in routine, or even something as simple as being hungry or tired. Recognizing your own common triggers is the first step toward managing the behavior that follows an emotion, rather than letting the emotion make the decision for you.

- **Anger** can lead to snapping at a friend or slamming a door — or, if managed, to calmly stating what upset you.
- **Anxiety or worry** can lead to avoiding a challenge entirely — or, if managed, to asking for help before a problem grows.
- **Excitement** can lead to an impulsive decision — or, if managed, to sharing enthusiasm while still thinking something through.
- **Sadness** can lead to withdrawing from friends and family — or, if managed, to reaching out and talking about what's wrong.

Notice the pattern: the emotion itself is never "bad." Every emotion is a normal, useful signal. What matters for health is the behavior that follows it, and that behavior can often be shaped with practice.

## Trusted Adult

You have now seen two outside influences (family, peers) and one inside influence (emotions) that shape your decisions. All three point toward the same practical need: sometimes a student needs support from someone older and more experienced. A **trusted adult** is a mature person a student can go to for help, honest information, or support — someone who listens without judgment and takes a student's concerns seriously. A trusted adult might be a parent or guardian, but it does not have to be. It could be a grandparent, an aunt or uncle, a teacher, a school counselor, a school nurse, a coach, or a family friend.

Not every adult in your life is automatically a trusted adult for every situation, and that is normal. A good trusted adult usually shares these qualities:

1. They listen carefully instead of immediately interrupting or dismissing your concern.
2. They keep your trust by not sharing what you tell them unnecessarily, except when your safety is at risk.
3. They stay calm, even when the topic is serious or uncomfortable.
4. They help you find a next step, rather than just telling you the problem is unimportant.
5. They are available — you can actually reach them when you need to.

It is smart to identify more than one trusted adult, because different situations may call for different people. A question about a physical symptom might go to a school nurse or a parent. A worry about a friendship might go to a school counselor or an older sibling. Having a short mental list ready — before you actually need it — makes it far easier to act quickly in the moment.

## Telling a Trusted Adult

Identifying a trusted adult solves only half the problem — the other half is actually telling them. **Telling a trusted adult** is the communication skill of clearly and effectively sharing a concern, question, or need with a trusted adult so that they understand the situation and can help. Many students know exactly who they would go to, but hesitate or struggle when the moment actually arrives, especially if the topic feels embarrassing or scary.

A few practical moves make telling a trusted adult easier and more effective:

- **Choose a good moment**, when the adult can actually focus on you, rather than in the middle of a rushed or distracted moment — though if something feels urgent, it is always okay to interrupt.
- **State the concern directly**, using a short, clear sentence rather than hinting and hoping they guess ("I'm worried about something" is a fine opener, but be ready to follow with specifics).
- **Use "I" statements** to describe your own experience and feelings ("I felt scared when..." rather than only describing what someone else did).
- **Ask for what you need**, whether that is advice, help solving a problem, or simply someone to listen.
- **Try again with a different adult** if the first person you tell does not respond helpfully — one unhelpful reaction does not mean the concern isn't worth sharing.

Before comparing a strong example to a weak one, keep those five moves in mind, since the table below is built directly from them.

| Weaker Approach | Stronger Approach |
|---|---|
| Hinting vaguely and hoping the adult notices | Stating the concern in a clear, short sentence |
| Waiting for the "perfect" moment indefinitely | Choosing a reasonably good moment, but speaking up sooner if it feels urgent |
| Blaming or accusing another person first | Using an "I" statement to describe your own experience |
| Giving up after one adult seems too busy to help | Trying a different trusted adult if the first attempt doesn't work |

    Telling a trusted adult feels easier with practice, even in low-stakes moments. Try practicing on something small and easy first — asking for help with a chore or a homework question — so the skill feels natural when something bigger comes up.

## Health Concern Reporting

The most important application of "telling a trusted adult" is **health concern reporting** — sharing a health concern or question, for yourself or for someone else, with a trusted adult so it can be addressed properly. This includes physical symptoms (a rash, a persistent headache, feeling unusually tired), emotional struggles (ongoing sadness, worry that won't go away), or a concern about a friend (noticing a friend seems withdrawn, or hearing a friend describe something unsafe).

Health concern reporting for someone else can feel more complicated than reporting for yourself, because a student may worry about breaking a friend's trust or "getting someone in trouble." A helpful way to think about it: telling a trusted adult about a friend's health concern is not betrayal — it is one of the most caring things a friend can do, because it connects that friend to help they may not be able to get for themselves.

**Situation:** During lunch, your friend Amara quietly mentions she hasn't been sleeping well for two weeks because she's been anxious about something at home, and asks you not to tell anyone.

**Weak response:** Saying nothing because Amara asked you not to tell, and hoping the problem goes away on its own.

**Stronger response:** Telling Amara gently that this sounds like something a trusted adult could really help with, then choosing a good moment to tell a trusted adult yourself (a school counselor is a strong fit here), stating the concern directly ("I'm worried about my friend Amara — she told me she hasn't been sleeping because she's anxious about something at home"), and asking what should happen next.

**Why it matters:** Amara's short-term wish for privacy is real, but ongoing sleep loss and anxiety are exactly the kind of health concern trusted adults are trained to help with. A caring friend routes the concern to someone who can actually help — that is health concern reporting in action.
