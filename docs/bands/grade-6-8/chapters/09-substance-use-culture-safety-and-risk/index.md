## Commercial Tobacco Addiction

This chapter opens with a distinction that deserves careful, respectful attention. **Commercial tobacco addiction** is the physical and psychological dependence that develops from using manufactured tobacco products — cigarettes, cigars, chewing tobacco, and nicotine vapes — that are designed, produced, and marketed by companies for profit.

Commercial tobacco products are deliberately engineered to be as addictive as possible. Manufacturers add chemicals that speed nicotine's delivery to the brain, adjust nicotine levels to create and sustain dependence, and add flavors that make products more appealing to first-time users, including adolescents. This is not an accidental side effect of a plant-based product — it is the result of intentional chemical and product design choices made by an industry, which is why commercial tobacco is considered one of the most addictive legal substances available.

The health consequences of commercial tobacco addiction are well documented and serious:

- Long-term damage to the lungs, heart, and blood vessels, with harm that begins accumulating from the earliest uses.
- A high likelihood of continued, difficult-to-quit use once dependence develops, because nicotine changes the brain's reward circuitry in ways that make quitting a genuine physical challenge, not just a matter of willpower.
- Elevated risk for the person using the product and, through secondhand smoke or aerosol, for people nearby.

## Traditional Tobacco And Plant Medicine Use

Understanding commercial tobacco addiction makes it possible to draw a clear, respectful line to a very different practice. **Traditional tobacco and plant medicine use** refers to the ceremonial, spiritual, and medicinal use of tobacco and other plants by Indigenous peoples and other cultural communities, practiced in ways that are fundamentally different from commercial tobacco products in purpose, form, and frequency.

For many Indigenous nations, tobacco is one of several sacred medicines, alongside plants such as sage, cedar, and sweetgrass depending on the nation and region. Traditional tobacco is typically:

- **Grown, harvested, or gifted with intention**, often blessed or prepared through specific cultural protocols rather than purchased as a manufactured consumer product.
- **Used in small, specific amounts** by designated community members — for example, offered in prayer, placed at a ceremony, or shared with an elder or knowledge keeper — rather than smoked or inhaled repeatedly throughout the day.
- **Connected to spiritual and community meaning**, such as giving thanks, marking an important life event, or requesting guidance, rather than used for recreation or to satisfy a chemical craving.

Public health organizations, including many Indigenous-led health programs, deliberately use the term "commercial tobacco" in their prevention messaging for exactly this reason: it lets health education target manufactured, engineered-for-addiction products without casting judgment on a sacred cultural and spiritual practice. Confusing the two — treating any tobacco use as automatically harmful, or assuming traditional use is simply commercial smoking with extra steps — is a common misunderstanding that this chapter directly corrects.

## Medication Storage And Disposal Safety

The chapter now shifts from tobacco to a safety skill nearly every household needs: handling medications responsibly. **Medication storage and disposal safety** means keeping medications secured in a way that prevents accidental or unauthorized use, and getting rid of unused or expired medications through methods that prevent them from being misused or harming the environment.

Two practical habits form the foundation of this skill:

1. **Secure storage** — medications, including OTC products, should be stored in their original containers, out of reach of young children, and ideally in a location that is not easily accessible to anyone who might misuse them. A locked cabinet or lockbox is the safest option for households with young children or anyone at risk of misuse.
2. **Proper disposal** — unused or expired medication should never be thrown in the household trash or flushed down the toilet unless the label specifically says to do so. Flushing can introduce medication into the water supply, and trash disposal can allow the medication to be found and misused by someone else. The safest disposal method is a **medication take-back program**, often available at pharmacies, police departments, or community collection events, which safely destroys unused medication.

## Reading Medication Labels

Safe storage and disposal work best when paired with the skill of actually reading what is on a medication label before it is ever used. **Reading medication labels** means knowing how to locate and interpret the dosage, warning, and interaction information printed on medication packaging so that a medication is used exactly as intended.

A medication label is not simply a formality — it contains several categories of information that each serve a specific safety purpose:

| Label Section | What It Tells You | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Active ingredient | The chemical that produces the medication's effect | Prevents accidentally doubling up on the same ingredient in two different products |
| Dosage instructions | How much to take, how often, and for how long | Following it exactly avoids both under-treatment and overdose |
| Warnings | Conditions, ages, or combinations to avoid | Flags real safety risks specific to that medication |
| Drug interactions | Other medications or substances not to combine it with | Prevents dangerous chemical interactions |
| Expiration date | The date after which the medication may not work as intended or may have degraded | Signals when a medication should be replaced, not used |

## Traditional Plant Medicine Practices

Medication safety in a modern household is one form of care for the body; many cultures also maintain a much older tradition of healing knowledge. **Traditional plant medicine practices** are the knowledge systems, developed and passed down over generations, that use specific plants for healing, wellness, and ceremonial purposes within a cultural or community context.

These practices exist across many cultures worldwide and are distinct from both commercial pharmaceuticals and commercial tobacco products discussed earlier in this chapter:

- **Knowledge is held and passed down by designated community members**, such as elders, healers, or knowledge keepers, who understand which plants to use, in what preparation, and for what purpose — this knowledge is often specific to a particular nation, region, or family lineage.
- **Preparation and use follow specific cultural protocols**, which may include ceremony, prayer, or specific timing tied to seasons or life events, rather than following a standardized manufactured dosage.
- **Many modern medications originated from traditional plant knowledge** — a well-documented example is the pain reliever aspirin, whose active compound traces back to willow bark, used medicinally by multiple cultures long before it was chemically isolated and manufactured.

Respecting traditional plant medicine practices means recognizing them as legitimate knowledge systems with real cultural and historical value, not as an "alternative" to be dismissed or a health risk to be lumped in with substance misuse. Traditional plant medicine practices carry generations of tested knowledge, held carefully by the people trained to use them — that is very different from misusing a substance, since intention, training, and cultural context are the key differences.

## Benefits Of Being Substance-Free

With the cultural distinctions established, the chapter now turns to a genuinely positive topic. **Benefits of being substance-free** are the documented physical, mental, social, and academic advantages a person experiences by not using alcohol, commercial tobacco/nicotine, cannabis, or other drugs during adolescence.

These benefits are concrete and measurable, not just a list of things avoided:

- **Better sleep quality** — sleep is disrupted by alcohol, nicotine, and cannabis, so a substance-free adolescent brain and body get the deep, restorative sleep needed for growth, memory consolidation, and mood regulation.
- **Stronger athletic performance** — lung capacity, coordination, and reaction time (all affected by substances discussed in the previous chapter) stay at their natural, undisrupted level.
- **Sharper academic focus** — a developing brain not exposed to substances that disrupt the prefrontal cortex and memory systems is better positioned to learn, concentrate, and retain information.
- **Healthier relationships** — decision-making, communication, and consent all depend on clear judgment, which substance use can compromise.
- **Full, undisrupted brain development** — since the prefrontal cortex continues developing into the mid-20s, staying substance-free during adolescence protects that developmental process at the exact time it matters most.

## Healthy Stress Management Without Substances

Knowing the benefits of being substance-free matters most when paired with real tools for handling stress, since stress is one of the most common reasons people turn to substances in the first place. **Healthy stress management without substances** refers to concrete, evidence-based strategies for coping with stress that do not involve alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, or other drugs.

Effective, accessible strategies include:

1. **Physical activity** — exercise reduces stress hormones and releases the body's own natural mood-lifting chemicals, with documented benefits after even short bouts of activity.
2. **Creative expression** — art, music, writing, and other creative outlets give stress a productive outlet and have been linked in research to improved mood and reduced anxiety.
3. **Talking to someone** — sharing what is stressful with a trusted adult, friend, or counselor is one of the most consistently effective stress-management strategies documented in adolescent health research.
4. **Mindfulness and breathing techniques** — simple practices like slow breathing, body scans, or brief meditation measurably lower physical stress markers such as heart rate.
5. **Cultural, community, and nature connection** — for many students, time spent in cultural or community practice, or simply time outdoors, provides a grounded, healthy way to reset during a stressful period.

## Substance Use And Injury Risk

The remainder of this chapter addresses a serious topic directly and honestly: how substance use connects to broader risks in a person's life, starting with physical injury. **Substance use and injury risk** describes the documented ways that alcohol, cannabis, and other drugs impair the coordination, reaction time, and judgment a person needs to stay physically safe.

Impairment affects the body in specific, measurable ways: reaction time slows, balance and coordination become less reliable, and judgment about risk — such as how fast is too fast, or how high is too high to jump from — becomes less accurate. Public health data consistently shows that these effects translate into real-world consequences, including a higher rate of falls, accidents, and injuries among people who are impaired compared to people who are not. This is one clear reason impaired driving, discussed in the previous chapter, is treated as a leading preventable cause of injury among teenagers specifically.

It is important to understand this connection accurately: injury risk rises because of documented, physical impairment — not because something is inherently wrong with a person who has used a substance. If you are ever in a situation where you or someone else has been using a substance and an injury occurs or feels likely, getting a trusted adult involved immediately is the safest and most protective response, and it is never the wrong choice to ask for help.

## Substance Use And Violence Risk

**Substance use and violence risk** describes the documented statistical association between substance use and involvement in violence, both as a factor connected to someone causing harm and as a factor connected to someone experiencing harm.

Researchers have found this connection in multiple directions. Impaired judgment and reduced impulse control — the same brain effects discussed throughout this chapter — make de-escalating a tense situation harder and can make a person less able to recognize when a situation is becoming dangerous, whether they are the one who has used a substance or the people around them have. This does not mean substance use directly causes violence in every case, or that every person who uses a substance will be involved in violence — the relationship is a documented statistical association, not a guarantee, and framing it otherwise would be inaccurate and unhelpful.

What matters most for you is knowing what protects against this risk. Avoiding situations where substances and escalating conflict are both present, having a plan to leave or call for help, and knowing that trusted adults and school or community resources exist specifically to support young people through unsafe situations are all part of the prevention approach this course has emphasized from the beginning. If you ever feel unsafe in a situation involving substance use and conflict, leaving and contacting a trusted adult is always the right call.

## Substance Use And Self-Harm Risk

**Substance use and self-harm risk** describes the documented connection between substance use and an increased likelihood of self-harm, arising because substances impair the same judgment and emotional-regulation systems that normally help a person manage difficult feelings safely.

Alcohol and other drugs are depressants or mood-altering substances that can intensify difficult emotions rather than resolve them, while simultaneously reducing the judgment a person would otherwise use to reach out for help or make a safe choice in a difficult moment. Research on adolescent mental health has identified this combination — intensified emotional distress paired with reduced judgment — as a genuine risk factor connecting substance use to self-harm.

This is exactly why the warning signs and help-seeking skills from earlier in this course matter here. If you or someone you know is struggling with difficult emotions, using a substance to cope, or showing warning signs of self-harm, the protective response is the same one emphasized throughout this textbook: tell a trusted adult, school counselor, or health professional right away. Reaching out is never an overreaction, and support and treatment are available and effective.

## Substance Use And Sexual Health Risk

**Substance use and sexual health risk** describes how alcohol and other drugs impair the judgment needed for safe sexual decision-making, including the capacity to give or recognize consent and the ability to reduce risk of STIs and unintended pregnancy.

This connection matters for a specific, documented reason: consent requires clear, voluntary communication and the ability to think through a decision, and impairment directly interferes with both. A person who is impaired may have a reduced capacity to clearly communicate or recognize consent, and impairment is also linked to a lower likelihood of using protective strategies discussed earlier in this course, such as barrier methods, which connects substance use to increased risk of STIs and unintended pregnancy as well.

Understanding this connection is protective, not shaming. It reinforces why the personal boundaries, consent, and health-access skills from earlier chapters matter in real situations, and why avoiding substances in situations involving sexual decision-making is one of the clearest ways to protect both your own health and another person's. If you are ever concerned about a situation involving substance use and sexual health — your own or someone else's — trusted adults, school health staff, and healthcare providers are available to help, without judgment, exactly as described in this course's chapter on healthcare access.
