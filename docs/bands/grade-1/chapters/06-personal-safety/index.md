### Healthy Technology Use: Rules for Screens and Devices

**Healthy technology use** means using phones, tablets, computers, and other devices in ways that are safe, respectful, and approved by a trusted adult. A Grade 1 student is starting to use technology more — for games, videos, or schoolwork — and healthy technology use means always checking with a grown-up first.

Here are the basic rules of healthy technology use for a Grade 1 student:

1. Ask a trusted adult before using a device you haven't used before.
2. Only use apps, games, or websites a trusted adult has approved.
3. Keep screen time balanced with active play, reading, and family time.
4. Tell a trusted adult right away if something on a screen feels confusing, scary, or makes you feel uncomfortable.

Talking points for read-aloud:

- Ask the class: "What is one device you use at home or school?"
- Explain that devices are tools, like a pencil or a ball — helpful when used the right way.
- Remind students that a trusted adult should always know what apps or games they are using.

### Sharing Information Safely: Asking Before You Share

**Sharing information safely** means understanding that personal details — like your name, address, phone number, or a photo of yourself — should never be given out online or to a stranger without asking permission first. This idea connects directly to **permission**, which students explored in Chapter 1: just as we ask permission before borrowing something, we ask permission before sharing information about ourselves.

A Grade 1 student doesn't need to memorize every kind of personal information. The simple rule is: if you're not sure whether something is safe to share, stop and ask a trusted adult first. A good habit to build: if a screen ever asks for a name, address, or a picture, that's the signal to pause and ask a trusted adult before doing anything else.

### Unsafe Behavior: Actions That Could Cause Harm

Students already learned about **safe behavior** in Chapter 1 — actions that protect people from harm, like wearing a seatbelt or looking both ways before crossing a street. **Unsafe behavior** is the opposite: an action that could hurt yourself or someone else, whether at home, at school, or out in the community.

Recognizing unsafe behavior is an important skill. Some unsafe behaviors are easy to spot, like running near a swimming pool. Others are less obvious, like following someone you don't know or playing with matches. A Grade 1 student is learning to notice unsafe behavior and to know when it's time to ask a trusted adult for help.

Here are examples of safe and unsafe behaviors side by side:

| Situation | Safe Behavior | Unsafe Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Crossing the street | Looking both ways and crossing with an adult | Running into the street without looking |
| Riding in a car | Wearing a seatbelt | Unbuckling the seatbelt while the car is moving |
| At the playground | Waiting your turn on the slide | Climbing up the slide the wrong way |
| Meeting someone new | Staying near a trusted adult | Walking away with someone you don't know |
| Around the kitchen | Asking an adult before using the stove | Touching a hot stove or sharp knife alone |

### School Emergency: When Something Unexpected Happens at School

A **school emergency** is an unexpected event at school — like a fire, severe weather, or another situation — that requires everyone to follow a special safety plan. School emergencies don't happen often, but schools prepare for them so that every student and adult knows exactly what to do to stay safe.

Common types of school emergencies a Grade 1 student may practice for include:

- Fire (requiring students to leave the building)
- Severe weather, like a tornado (requiring students to move to a safe indoor spot)
- A situation requiring students to stay quietly in their classroom

Talking points for read-aloud:

- Ask the class: "Has our school ever practiced what to do during a fire drill?"
- Explain that adults at school have a plan for every kind of emergency, and the plan is practiced so it becomes easy to remember.
- Reassure students that the calm, orderly practice is what keeps everyone safe — there is no need to feel scared during a drill.

### Safety Drill: Practicing What to Do

A **safety drill** is a planned practice of what to do during a specific type of school emergency, done regularly so that everyone knows the steps by heart before a real emergency ever happens. Just like practicing a fire drill helps students walk calmly to a safe spot, practicing other drills helps students know exactly where to go and what to do.

Before we look at how a safety drill works step by step, let's define the key term: a **cue** is the signal — like an alarm sound or a teacher's instruction — that tells everyone a drill (or a real emergency) is starting.

## Body Safety: Your Body Belongs to You

The rest of this chapter covers **body safety** — one of the most important safety topics a Grade 1 student will learn. Please read this section carefully before teaching it, and keep the tone calm, warm, and matter-of-fact.

**Body safety** means understanding that your own body belongs to you, and that you have the right to say how, when, and by whom your body is touched. This idea builds directly on something students already learned: every body part has a correct name, and a person's body belongs to them.

Three simple ideas make up body safety for a Grade 1 student:

1. Your body belongs to you.
2. Most touches are safe and welcome — like a hug from a family member or a high-five from a friend — but you always get to decide what feels okay for your own body.
3. If a touch ever feels wrong, confusing, or makes you uncomfortable, you can say "no," and you should always tell a trusted adult about it, right away.

### Unsafe Touch: When Something Doesn't Feel Right

An **unsafe touch** is any touch that makes a person feel confused, scared, or uncomfortable, or that involves the private parts of the body covered by a swimsuit. This definition stays simple and clear for a Grade 1 student, without graphic detail: an unsafe touch is one that feels wrong, and a student's feelings about their own body are always worth listening to.

It helps to know that an unsafe touch can come from anyone — even someone the student knows and normally trusts, like a family member, neighbor, or older student. This is why the rule stays the same no matter who is involved: if a touch feels wrong, it's an unsafe touch, and it should always be reported to a trusted adult.

A simple way to help students hold onto this idea is the "uh-oh feeling" — the uncomfortable feeling in your stomach or chest that shows up when something isn't right. Students already have practice naming feelings from earlier chapters; body safety asks them to notice that same "uh-oh feeling" specifically in connection with touch, and to act on it by telling a trusted adult.

### Saying No: Your Right to Refuse an Unsafe Touch

**Saying no** is the clear, direct way a student can refuse an unsafe touch or an unsafe situation. A Grade 1 student is old enough to practice saying "no" firmly, even to an adult, and even to someone they know well. This is an important shift from earlier grades: students are now expected to actually **demonstrate** this skill, not just talk about it.

Saying no can look like:

- Saying the word "No" in a clear, firm voice
- Saying "Stop, I don't like that" while stepping away
- Simply walking away and going straight to a trusted adult

A student never needs a perfect reason to say no to a touch that doesn't feel right — feeling uncomfortable is reason enough. Practicing this skill out loud, in a safe classroom setting, helps a student feel ready to use it in real life.

### Asking A Trusted Adult: The Most Important Step

Every idea in this chapter leads to the same final step: **asking a trusted adult** for help. Students met the idea of a trusted adult in Chapter 1 — someone at home, at school, or in the community who a student can go to for help. In body safety, asking a trusted adult is not optional or just one choice among many — it is the step that must always happen, every single time a touch feels unsafe or confusing.

Three things are true every time a student tells a trusted adult about an unsafe touch, and these are worth repeating often:

- It is never the student's fault.
- The student will not be in trouble for telling.
- The trusted adult's job is to listen, believe the student, and help keep them safe.

If the first trusted adult a student tells doesn't help, the student should keep telling other trusted adults until someone helps. This is an important backup rule: telling is always the right choice, and a student should never stop at just one try.

### Wrap-Up for Teachers

This chapter connected nine ideas across two themes. The first theme covered everyday safety skills: healthy technology use, sharing information safely by asking permission first, telling safe behavior from unsafe behavior, and understanding school emergencies and the safety drills that prepare everyone for them. The second theme, body safety, is the most important part of this chapter: every student's body belongs to them, an unsafe touch is any touch that feels wrong, saying "no" is always allowed, and asking a trusted adult is the step that must always happen. By the end of this chapter, most Grade 1 students should be able to demonstrate saying "no" to an unwanted touch, name at least three trusted adults they could tell, and explain that telling a trusted adult is never something they'll get in trouble for. Please revisit these ideas throughout the year in short, calm check-ins rather than treating this as a one-time lesson.
