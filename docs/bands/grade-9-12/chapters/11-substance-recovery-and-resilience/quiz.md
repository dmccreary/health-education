#### 1. What does naloxone do during an opioid overdose?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It permanently cures opioid use disorder with a single dose
2. It can temporarily reverse the effects of an opioid overdose by restoring normal breathing within minutes
3. It reverses overdoses from any substance, including alcohol and stimulants
4. It prevents the need to ever call 911 during an overdose
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Naloxone is a medication, often given as a nasal spray, that can temporarily reverse the effects of an opioid overdose by restoring normal breathing within minutes. It has no effect on non-opioid overdoses, and its effects are temporary, which is why calling 911 remains essential even after it is used.

#### 3. Why do Good Samaritan laws matter for overdose response, according to the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. They protect a person who calls 911 to report an overdose from prosecution for drug possession, addressing a documented reason people hesitate to call for help
2. They only protect medical professionals, not bystanders
3. They eliminate the need to call 911 in an overdose situation
4. They require every caller to identify the substance involved before help arrives
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter explains that Good Samaritan laws, in place in most states, protect a caller, including the person experiencing the overdose, from being prosecuted for drug possession based on evidence from that call. These laws exist because fear of legal consequences is a documented reason people hesitate to call for help during an overdose.

#### 7. A person shows rapid, irregular heartbeat, extremely high body temperature, and agitation. Based on the chapter's overdose sign categories, how does this presentation differ from an opioid overdose?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is identical to an opioid overdose in every respect
2. It reflects a stimulant overdose, which speeds the body up, unlike an opioid overdose, which slows breathing and produces pinpoint pupils and a bluish skin tone
3. Opioid overdose also always includes extremely high body temperature
4. Pinpoint pupils are a defining feature of stimulant overdose, not opioid overdose
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter distinguishes stimulant overdose, which presents with rapid or irregular heartbeat, extremely high body temperature, and agitation because stimulants speed the body up, from opioid overdose, which presents with slow or stopped breathing, pinpoint pupils, and a bluish skin tone because opioids suppress the drive to breathe.

#### 10. A classmate argues that since naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose, calling 911 afterward isn't really necessary. Evaluating this claim using the chapter's evidence, what is the strongest response?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The classmate is correct, since naloxone permanently resolves the overdose once administered
2. Naloxone should be used instead of calling 911 whenever it is available
3. Calling 911 is only useful if naloxone is not available at all
4. Naloxone's effects are temporary and the opioid can outlast them, so the person can slip back into overdose, and 911 also ensures monitoring and follow-up care that naloxone alone cannot provide
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter directly addresses this claim: naloxone's effects are temporary, while the opioid causing the overdose can remain in the body longer, meaning a person can slip back into overdose after appearing to recover. Calling 911 ensures trained responders can monitor breathing and provide follow-up care that naloxone alone cannot, which is why the response sequence always places calling 911 first.

    **Concept Tested:** Overdose Emergency Response
