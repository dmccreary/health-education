## Influences On Health Behavior

Every day, you make choices about how to act, what to eat, how to spend your time, and how to treat other people. Very few of those choices happen in a vacuum — most are shaped by people and forces around you, whether or not you notice it.

**Influences on health behavior** are the people, groups, and forces — including family, peers, culture, and media/technology — that shape the choices someone makes about their health and safety. Your family teaches you habits from a young age, like how to brush your teeth or how to talk about feelings. Your friends can encourage you to try a new sport or, sometimes, pressure you toward a choice you are not sure about. Your culture shapes what foods feel familiar and what traditions feel comforting. Media and technology — shows, apps, games, and social platforms — show you images and ideas that can shape what you think is normal or expected.

None of these influences are automatically good or bad. Recognizing that an influence exists is the first step toward deciding, on purpose, whether to follow it.

The following table organizes the four major influences this chapter focuses on, along with one example of each:

| Influence | Example |
|---|---|
| Family | A parent who models washing hands before meals |
| Peers | A friend who invites you to join a walking club at recess |
| Culture | A community tradition that shapes favorite foods or celebrations |
| Media/technology | A video or app that shows a certain way of talking or acting as "normal" |

## Media And Technology Influence

Media and technology deserve their own closer look, because they reach students constantly and in ways that are easy to miss.

**Media and technology influence** is the way that shows, videos, games, apps, advertisements, and social media shape a person's ideas, habits, and behavior — sometimes without the person even realizing it is happening. An advertisement might make a sugary snack look exciting. A video game might make staying up late feel normal. A social media post might make an unhealthy habit look like something "everyone" does, even when that is not true.

Media and technology are not automatically harmful — many shows, games, and apps are entertaining, educational, or help people stay connected to friends and family. The goal is not to avoid all media and technology, but to notice its influence and think critically about it.

Here are questions that help you think critically about media and technology influence:

- Is this trying to sell me something?
- Does this show a healthy choice, or just an exciting one?
- Would a trusted adult agree this is a good idea?
- Does this make an unhealthy or unsafe choice look normal?

## Harmful Technology Behavior

Media and technology can also be used in ways that directly hurt someone's safety or emotional well-being. This next idea is more serious, so it deserves careful, direct attention.

**Harmful technology behavior** is any use of a phone, app, game, or the internet that hurts someone's safety or mental health — including cyberbullying, sharing someone's photos or information without permission, or a stranger online trying to get personal information or arrange to meet in person. This kind of behavior can happen through texting, gaming chat, social media, or any app that lets people communicate.

Some warning signs that a technology situation may be harmful include:

1. Someone online asks for your address, your school's name, or a photo they should not need
2. A person online asks you to keep a conversation secret from your parents or another trusted adult
3. Messages or posts are being used to embarrass, threaten, or exclude someone repeatedly
4. Someone asks to meet in person after only talking online
5. A message or image makes you feel confused, scared, or uneasy in your stomach

If any of these things happen to you or a friend, the response is always the same, no matter which situation it is: stop responding, do not delete the message, and tell a trusted adult right away. You will never be in trouble for reporting something that felt wrong online — telling an adult is always the safe and correct choice.

## Situational Awareness

Just as you can learn to notice a harmful pattern online, you can also learn to notice safety clues in the physical world around you.

**Situational awareness** means paying attention to your surroundings, the people nearby, and how a situation is unfolding, so you can notice early if something feels unsafe. It is not about being afraid all the time — it is a calm habit of checking in with your surroundings, similar to looking both ways before crossing a street.

Building situational awareness includes noticing things like:

- Who else is around, and whether a trusted adult is nearby
- Whether you know a safe way out of the area you are in
- Whether the mood of a situation is calm or is starting to feel tense
- Whether your own body is giving you a signal, like a nervous stomach or a strong urge to leave

## Exiting Unsafe Situations

Noticing a warning clue only helps if you also know what to do next. That is exactly what this next skill covers.

**Exiting unsafe situations** means having a clear, practiced plan for leaving a place, conversation, or group as soon as something feels unsafe or wrong, without waiting to see if it gets worse. Trusting that uneasy feeling in your stomach, and acting on it right away, is one of the most important safety skills a person can build.

A simple plan for exiting an unsafe situation includes these steps:

1. **Notice the feeling.** Your body often signals unsafe before your mind fully understands why.
2. **State that you are leaving, if it is safe to speak.** "I have to go now" is enough — you do not owe anyone a detailed explanation.
3. **Move toward people or a place you know is safe** — a trusted adult, a public area, or home.
4. **Tell a trusted adult what happened**, even if you are not fully sure it was actually unsafe. It is always okay to check.

You never need a "good enough" reason to leave a situation that feels wrong. Trusting your own instinct is reason enough.

    Try saying "I have to go now" out loud a few times at home. Practicing the words ahead of time makes them much easier to use for real, if you ever need them.

## Personal Boundaries

Exiting an unsafe situation is one way of protecting yourself. Setting a boundary before things go that far is another — and it is worth understanding clearly.

**Personal boundaries** are the limits a person sets about what they are comfortable with regarding their body, their belongings, their time, and their information. Boundaries are personal — what feels comfortable for one person may not feel comfortable for another, and that is completely normal. Everyone has the right to their own boundaries, and everyone has a responsibility to respect other people's boundaries too.

Boundaries can apply to many parts of life, including:

| Type of Boundary | Example |
|---|---|
| Physical | Deciding who can hug you or how close someone can stand |
| Belongings | Deciding whether to lend an item to someone |
| Time | Deciding whether to join an activity right now or later |
| Information | Deciding what personal details to share, and with whom |

A boundary is not rude or unfriendly — it is a normal, healthy part of every relationship, including friendships and family relationships.

## Setting Boundaries

Understanding that boundaries exist is the first step. Actually stating one, clearly and respectfully, is the practical skill that follows.

**Setting boundaries** means clearly and respectfully telling another person what you are, or are not, comfortable with. A boundary does not need to be angry or dramatic — it can be calm, short, and direct.

Here are example phrases for setting a boundary in different situations:

- "Please ask before you borrow my things."
- "I'm not ready to talk about that yet."
- "Please don't touch my hair without asking."
- "I need some quiet time right now."

Setting a boundary sometimes feels uncomfortable at first, especially with a friend or family member, but a boundary stated calmly and respectfully is one of the healthiest things you can do for a relationship — it helps both people understand each other better. Setting your own boundaries matters, and so does respecting someone else's boundary the moment they state it, even if you don't fully understand why they set it.

## Warning Sign

Personal boundaries and situational awareness both depend on noticing clues. This next concept names exactly what those clues look like when a person or a situation is unsafe.

A **warning sign** is a behavior or detail that signals a person or situation might be unsafe or dangerous. Warning signs do not guarantee danger, but they are a signal to slow down, pay closer attention, and consider getting help from a trusted adult.

Warning signs can appear in people's words, actions, or requests. Examples include:

1. **Asking you to keep a secret from your parents or another trusted adult**
2. **Ignoring you when you say no or ask them to stop**
3. **Offering gifts, favors, or special attention that feels like it comes with a hidden expectation**
4. **Trying to get you alone, away from other people**
5. **Making you feel confused about whether something is okay, when your gut says it isn't**

Noticing one warning sign does not always mean danger is certain, but it always means it is time to pay closer attention and consider telling a trusted adult about what you noticed.

    Answer: Yes, this is a warning sign — asking a child to keep a secret from a trusted adult is one of the clearest warning signs there is. The safe response is to tell a trusted adult about the gift and the request to keep it secret, right away.

## Peer Pressure

Not every unsafe situation involves a stranger or a clear danger. Sometimes the pressure comes from someone you already know — even a friend — and it can be much harder to notice.

**Peer pressure** is the feeling of being pushed, directly or indirectly, by people your own age to do something you might not otherwise choose to do. Peer pressure is not always loud or obvious — sometimes it is a comment like "everyone else is doing it," and sometimes it is simply the discomfort of being the only person who says no.

Peer pressure can push someone toward a harmless choice, like trying a new food, or toward an unsafe or unhealthy choice, like skipping a safety rule, being unkind to someone else, or trying something that could hurt them. Recognizing which kind of pressure you are feeling is an important first step.

## Refusal Skill

This is exactly the plan you need: a clear, practiced way to say no and mean it.

A **refusal skill** is a specific, practiced way of saying no to pressure, clearly and confidently, so that your choice is respected. A refusal skill is not the same as simply hoping you will know what to say in the moment — it is a script, practiced ahead of time, so the words are ready when you need them.

Here are refusal skill scripts you can practice right now, out loud, so they become automatic:

- **The direct no:** "No thanks, I'm not doing that." Then walk away.
- **The broken record:** Repeat the same short refusal calmly, no matter what the other person says. "No thanks." "No thanks." "No thanks." You do not need a new excuse each time — repeating the same short phrase is often the most effective response.
- **Suggest something else:** "No thanks, let's play basketball instead." Offering an alternative can redirect the group without an argument.
- **Blame a rule or trusted adult:** "I can't, my mom said I need to be home by five." Using a family rule as the reason takes the pressure off of you personally.
- **Walk away:** Leaving the situation entirely, especially if the pressure continues after you have said no.

A refusal skill does not require an explanation, an apology, or a debate. "No thanks" is a complete sentence.

## Demonstrating Refusal Skills

Knowing a refusal script and actually being able to use it in the moment are two different things. This final section is about practicing until the script feels natural.

**Demonstrating refusal skills** means actually using a refusal script out loud, in a realistic situation, until it feels natural and confident rather than awkward or forced. Just like practicing a fire drill helps you react quickly in a real emergency, practicing refusal scripts with a trusted adult, family member, or classmate helps you use them confidently when you actually need them.
